**Latest, October 25:** The case now has would-be defendants on both tracks. The ACLU and the ACLU of Alaska moved on Friday to intervene for two Alaska voters, the League of Women Voters of Alaska and Common Cause, filing a proposed motion to dismiss and, on one account of the filing, asking the court to consider sanctions against the department's lawyers. Common Cause called the suit an attempt to lay the groundwork to reject election results, and the ACLU calls it frivolous and lawless, resting on a single claim built on the National Voter Registration Act's purpose language. Separately, Alaskans for Mary and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee also moved to intervene, calling the suit an unprecedented pre-emptive strike brought on the president's personal orders. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, asked about the case, said the lawsuit is not trying to stop the certification of Alaska's election, a narrower public description than the complaint's request to block certification of any result that may be impacted by voter confusion pending an investigation.

**Latest:** President Donald Trump confirmed on Friday that he had personally ordered the legal action. I'm the one who said do it, Trump told reporters in Florida, acknowledging his direct involvement in the Justice Department's suit, The Hill reported. Trump had foreshadowed the case on Wednesday, calling Daniel J. Sullivan's candidacy a scam on Truth Social and saying powerful legal options were being explored to remove him from the ballot. Democrat Mary Peltola called the lawsuit a desperate attempt for Republicans to cling to power, the senator's campaign said it would let the complaint proceed and that Alaskans can tell the candidates apart, and Alaska election officials said they were reviewing the lawsuit while remaining focused on running the election. Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired teacher from Petersburg listed without a party label, has denied any connection to Democrats and said he is running because he is dissatisfied with the incumbent's record; the Alaska Supreme Court previously allowed him to remain on the ballot after election officials tried to remove him.

The legal foundation is drawing sharp criticism. The Election Law Blog's reading of the complaint in United States v. Alaska is that the department cites the preamble to the National Voter Registration Act, its findings and purposes language about protecting the integrity of the electoral process, as the source of the duty it says certification would breach, excising the purposes of this chapter are framing in the process, and that the case is not yet ripe because no votes have been cast in the general election. The New York Times reported that legal experts see the challenge as dubious on similar grounds. The department asks the court to prevent certification of any result that may be impacted by voter confusion pending an investigation into whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant for Senator Sullivan.

**Latest, October 26:** Attorney General Todd Blanche took the department's case to the Sunday shows, appearing on CNN's State of the Union and CBS's Face the Nation. He said the suit aims to put guardrails in place so ballots are preserved in a way that lets a court later judge whether confusion between Senator Dan Sullivan and Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired schoolteacher from Petersburg, swung the race. Pressed by CNN's Dana Bash on whether the administration was preparing ground for President Trump to reverse an unwelcome outcome, Blanche said it was not laying any groundwork for the president to do that and that Trump was not trying to seize control of a state election in any way. He accused the second Sullivan of intentionally trying to confuse voters, a charge Daniel J. Sullivan has denied. Separately, Democratic candidate Mary Peltola delayed campaign stops after a family car crash, The Guardian reported on Sunday. No court ruling on the suit or on the intervention motions had been reported by Monday morning.

The suit, filed in US District Court in Alaska, asks a judge to declare that certifying a result affected by voter confusion would breach the state's duties under the National Voter Registration Act, CNBC reported. It quotes Alaska Elections Director Carol Beecher's finding that Daniel J. Sullivan's candidacy, which took 2.5 percent in the August 28 open primary, was for the purpose of confusing or misleading voters, and argues that his votes would transfer to Peltola under ranked choice voting after his elimination. Peltola leads Sullivan by a narrow two points in the FiftyPlusOne polling average cited by CNBC. The race is one of seven Senate toss ups rated by Cook Political Report, and polls close on November 3.
