**Latest, October 25:** Brazil's Supreme Court extended voting in the presidential runoff nationwide by two hours, to 7 p.m. Brasilia time, minutes after polls had been due to close at 5 p.m., and electoral authorities that had briefly begun counting paused the count, Bloomberg reported. Justice Cristiano Zanin issued the order in Constitutional Complaint 101.673, brought by Lula's Brasil Pronto Pra Mais coalition against the city governments of Salvador, Feira de Santana and Lauro de Freitas in Bahia, TMC reported. The complaint said bus provision in the three cities was insufficient to get voters to polling stations and alleged a breach of the court's binding ruling in ADPF 1013 on free public transport on election days. Zanin wrote that the facts had reached the court at 4:32 p.m. Brasilia time and cited their seriousness and the evident urgency of the measure; he extended the receipt of votes in every polling station in the country, not only in the cities named, and made the Superior Electoral Court responsible for carrying the order out. The decision records no count of the buses that did not run and assigns no individual responsibility for the failures. Lula's coalition had reported transport failures in nine other states, including Sao Paulo, where it said the fleet ran at about 60 percent, and Rio de Janeiro, and Lula had told reporters his camp would not be robbed by mayors, governors or business owners. Flavio Bolsonaro's campaign had also asked for voting to be extended, in municipalities in Sao Paulo, Parana and Santa Catarina affected by rain, flooding and road closures, Lex Legal reported. Voting now ends at 7 p.m. Brasilia time, and the national count follows the extended close.

**Latest, October 25:** Bahia's regional electoral court (TRE-BA) ordered the arrest in flagrante of Salvador's mobility secretary, Pablo Souza, and the immediate restoration of the city's full bus fleet at weekday frequency, fare free, under a fine of R$1 million per hour on the municipality, the secretariat and the concession companies. The court's president, Mauricio Kertzman Szporer, acted after the Federacao Brasil da Esperanca, the PT-led federation backing Lula, complained at about 10:30 a.m. that Salvador's buses were running at less than half the normal weekday fleet and filed video and photographs of vehicles held in garages during voting hours, citing Electoral Code articles 297 and 304. At least 75 buses were prevented from leaving garages, and Bahia's consumer agency Procon-BA found 55 buses parked but fit to operate at one operator's garage and cited the company. Salvador's mayor, Bruno Reis, said the held buses belonged to one consortium and left only after union members removed stickers bearing the number 13, Lula's ballot number, which he called an electoral crime; the mobility secretariat said Souza had ordered that no bus be retained and that the operation was being normalised. The court gave the city one hour from notification to prove compliance and sent the case to the regional electoral prosecutor and the Federal Police. The dispute was not confined to Salvador: Lula's campaign petitioned the electoral court in Sao Paulo on Sunday saying the city's buses were running at only 60 percent of the fleet, in breach of a court order for full operation, and asked that the city government and SPTrans be summoned to restore service at once; Sao Paulo's city hall had not commented, Folha reported. The PT was also submitting a request to the TSE, the national electoral court, to extend voting beyond the 5 p.m. close, and said it would seek the detention of Sao Paulo officials as well; no TSE decision had been reported.

Brazilians voted on Sunday in the presidential runoff that closes a campaign this site has tracked since the first round on October 4, choosing between President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, seeking a fourth term, and Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the eldest son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, who finished the first round ahead with 47.03 percent of valid votes to Lula's 45.16 percent.

The final polls left the race inside every margin of error. A Reuters review of the surveys published on Saturday put Bolsonaro narrowly ahead in all five: CNT/MDA had him at 46.3 percent to Lula's 45.0, from 2,032 interviews on October 21 to 23; Datafolha had 48 to 47, from 4,050 interviews on October 23 to 24; Quaest had 46 to 45, from 3,702 interviews; AtlasIntel had 49.9 to 48.4, from 4,990 interviews on October 18 to 23; and Nexus had 47.0 to 46.0, from 2,000 interviews. Each pollster stated a margin of error of one to 2.2 points, so none of the leads is statistically distinguishable from a tie.

Friday night's final Globo debate, covered here yesterday, turned on living costs, welfare and corruption, and both camps spent Saturday at rallies rather than in new policy announcements. Turnout is the variable the campaigns themselves stressed: abstention was high in the first round, and Datafolha's turnout modelling pointed to a slightly wider Bolsonaro edge among the voters most likely to cast a ballot.

Polls close at 5 p.m. Brasilia time and the electoral court begins publishing the national count immediately, with Brazil's electronic system expected to produce a result within hours. What to watch tonight is not only the winner but the loser's first statement: both camps have pre-positioned dispute narratives through the campaign, and Argentina's President Milei, who has feuded openly with Lula, is the regional leader with the most riding on the outcome.
