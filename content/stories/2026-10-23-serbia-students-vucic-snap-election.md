**Latest, October 25:** Local election monitors recorded cases of photographing ballots that suggested pressure on voters, attempted vote buying and carousel voting, the Associated Press reported, as the state election commission put turnout at about 35 percent by noon, against 21 percent at the same hour in 2023. Vucic said his camp would announce reliable projections about three hours after polls close at 8 p.m. local time, warned that any protests claiming fraud if his party won would be immediately quashed, and the commission is not expected to release the official tally before Monday.

**Latest, October 25:** Turnout reached 35.1 percent by noon local time, according to Ipsos research data published by the Telegraf portal and reported by TASS, against 21.33 percent at the same hour in the 2023 election. The Republican Electoral Commission registered 6,451,292 voters for 250 seats, with more than 16,000 observers monitoring the vote, including 622 from abroad, TASS reported.

**Latest, October 25:** The ruling Serbian Progressive Party said a group of young people it described as protest supporters pushed Vucic's mother, Angelina Vucic, shouted at her and tore grocery bags she was carrying from a market, and said she had reported the incident to police. TASS, which reported the party statement, gave no location for the incident and no police confirmation or comment from any other party, and the claim is carried here as the ruling party's statement only.

**Latest, October 25:** Turnout reached 12 percent by 9 a.m., pollster CeSID estimated, more than double the same hour in the previous election in 2023, Reuters reported, and even Vucic waited in line for nearly two hours to vote. After voting, Vucic said Serbia would choose its direction on Sunday, adding: "If we lose we will acknowledge the will of the electorate, if we win no one will take it (from us)." In Novi Sad, Ilija Srdanovic, who leads the student list, said he hoped the day would pass in a democratic atmosphere and that the scenes suggested a record turnout in the country's recent history.

**Latest, October 25:** Unusually long lines formed at polling stations in Belgrade and Novi Sad before voting opened at 7 a.m., Reuters reported from Belgrade, with voters telling its reporters they had come out early because the students called them. Reuters put the student list's support in the context of nearly two years of demonstrations, and noted that many voters credit Vucic with a period of modernisation, including highways, railways, schools and hospitals. Polls close at 8 p.m. local time, with first indications expected about two hours later.

**Latest:** Polling stations opened at 7 a.m. local time on Sunday and close at 8 p.m., with first indications of the result expected about two hours after the close, Al Jazeera reported. A final Ipsos poll published on Thursday put the Serbian Progressive Party at 47 percent and the student list at 40 percent, although other polls have put the students ahead. Neither Al Jazeera nor Deutsche Welle reported a turnout figure, an election day incident or an observer comment by midday in Belgrade. Analyst Jelena Vasiljevic of the University of Belgrade told Al Jazeera that turnout would decide the contest, because the governing party holds a disciplined core vote while the student list needs opposition voters, habitual nonvoters and the undecided to turn out together. Both reports said Vucic resigned as president in September, after calling the snap election, to run again for prime minister.

Serbia's student movement took its two-year protest campaign into a final mass rally on Thursday, filling Belgrade's Slavija Square and surrounding streets three days before a snap parliamentary election that pits the Student List against President Aleksandar Vucic's Serbian Progressive Party. The rally, called Victory Calling, capped multi-day marches in which students walked to the capital from across the country, and candidates promised a judiciary and prosecution free of political pressure and education and healthcare equally available to all.

The movement began after a concrete canopy collapsed at Novi Sad railway station in November 2024, killing 16 people. Protesters blamed corruption and failures in construction oversight, and the campaign widened from demands for a transparent investigation into a nationwide anti-corruption movement calling for early elections and institutional reform. The list operates without a single leader and has avoided detailed foreign policy commitments in order to hold together left, centre and right-leaning voters.

Vucic held his own closing rally at a sports hall in New Belgrade the same evening. Analysts cited in the coverage say a student-led government would face immediate domestic priorities before foreign policy, though its candidates have taken a hard line on Kosovo, proposed reviewing agreements reached under EU auspices and floated a referendum on EU membership within six months of taking office. Moscow has portrayed the protests as a Western-backed colour revolution, while the United States Congress has voiced concern about democratic standards and Serbia's ties with Russia.

What is at stake economically is the direction of a candidate country whose EU accession has stalled over rule of law concerns and limited alignment with EU sanctions, including those against Russia. A change of government would not automatically transform that position, but democratic reform, judicial independence and anti-corruption enforcement are the conditions Brussels has set for reviving the process. Polls close on Sunday, October 25.
