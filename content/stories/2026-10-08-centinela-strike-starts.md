The strike at Centinela, one of Chile's largest copper operations, began Wednesday morning after mediation failed last week (covered October 5). More than 700 workers across two unions downed tools in a dispute over pay and benefit equalization with other Antofagasta operations. The Minera Esperanza and Distrito Centinela unions, with 708 members combined, voted 98.73 percent in favor of the strike on September 28. RBC analysts have flagged that inventories outside the United States are already tight, and supervisors at BHP's Escondida, the world's largest copper mine, have also voted to strike, widening the Chilean supply risk.

Day two brought the price response. Benchmark three-month copper on the LME rose 1 percent to $14,614.50 a tonne in early Thursday trading after touching $14,646.50, its highest in nearly two weeks, as Chinese traders returned from the week-long National Day holiday. BMO Capital Markets said in a Thursday note that the strike has disrupted mining, haulage and mine development activities, though processing plants can continue operating in the near term; the impact on copper production could become more visible from November if the stoppage continues. The unions say they represent more than half of the mine's operators and over 60 percent of its workers, that the plants are still being fed but material movement has largely stopped, and that output will fall step by step because the mine must move large amounts of waste to reach the ore.

By Friday the metal was consolidating just below record levels. Bloomberg reported copper rose 1.2 percent to $14,474.50 a tonne in Singapore on Friday, set to finish the week about 1.5 percent higher, with a gauge of Chinese import demand hitting a four-year high as buyers restock after the holiday. StoneX analysts said they remain constructive on copper, supported by supply risks, seasonal Chinese demand and continued tariff uncertainty. Antofagasta said it remains willing to negotiate and does not expect the disruption to change its production outlook; its shares fell 0.8 percent to 36.64 pence in London on Thursday morning, extending Wednesday's 3.6 percent decline, for a market value of about 36.1 billion pounds.

The dispute entered its fourth day on Saturday with the core demand now clear: benefit parity across unions. The Minera Esperanza and Distrito Centinela unions want equal benefits for workers doing similar jobs regardless of which union they belong to, and say the company refused to discuss equalizing them. Antofagasta argues that since 2020, bargaining schedules have been separated and wage increases negotiated at different times. The company's offer was a 2 percent real increase in base pay plus a bonus of 22.5 million Chilean pesos (about $22,900) per worker, which the unions rejected; the strike vote passed with 98.7 percent support on September 28. This is the first strike at Centinela and only the second in Antofagasta's history. A broker note from GEM Mining Consulting, cited in market commentary Friday, estimated about 5 percent of Chilean copper production is at risk from the disruption; that figure is an analyst claim, not independently verified.
