Nigeria’s minister of solid minerals development, Dele Alake, inaugurated the Nigeria United States Committee on Strategic Cooperation in the Solid Minerals Sector in Abuja on October 27, and used the occasion to reject reports that Nigeria had sold 700 billion dollars worth of solid minerals to the United States. The 700 billion dollar figure, carried in several national dailies, described an estimate of the value of Nigeria’s mining sector under the cooperation framework the two governments agreed in New York during the 81st session of the United Nations General Assembly, not a transaction price.

Alake was explicit about the structure. “There is no sale of any Nigerian asset. There’s nothing exchanging hands,” he said. The memorandum of understanding is a government to government platform designed to facilitate business to business engagements between American investors and Nigerian local operators, he said, adding that the document was signed in public and published in four or five national newspapers. He described reports that the United States intended to take over Nigeria’s minerals or its security architecture as untrue at best and a product of riotous imagination at worst.

The policy content sits underneath the denial. Alake said the government has made local processing mandatory, refusing to grant mineral titles to investors without a clear pathway to processing in Nigeria, and presented the partnership as a route to investment, technology transfer, local processing and jobs under President Bola Tinubu’s economic diversification agenda. The United States embassy’s economic officer, Jonathan Baas, said President Donald Trump had prioritised energy, artificial intelligence and critical minerals as focus areas for American diplomatic missions.

Jurisdiction watch: Nigeria is competing for the same Western critical minerals capital as the Democratic Republic of Congo and West African gold and lithium jurisdictions, but on stated terms that keep ownership Nigerian and push processing onshore. The committee’s first test is whether its business to business pipeline produces financed projects under those terms, or whether the 700 billion dollar misunderstanding becomes the domestic political frame for every deal it touches.
