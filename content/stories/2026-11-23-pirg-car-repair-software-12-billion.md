Fixing a modern car increasingly means buying permission to talk to it. A report titled Closing Shop, from the US Public Interest Research Group, estimates that independent repair shops spend a collective 83 million hours a year buying, downloading, logging into and waiting on manufacturer software, the working time of about 42,000 technicians, worth roughly $12 billion at a shop rate of $140 an hour. About one in five repairs at independent shops needs manufacturer software, and on those jobs the surveyed mechanics spent an estimated 1.25 hours on software administration alone. In August 2026 the researchers priced a single seat of software access across five major automakers at $15,878 a year.

The report says 88 percent of surveyed mechanics turned away a repair in the past year because they could not access or afford the software, pushing drivers toward authorised dealerships, which in Massachusetts data charged 36 percent more than independents for the same work in 2020. Because higher repair costs feed insurance claims, PIRG argues drivers pay for the lock-in through premiums whether or not their own car is repaired.

PIRG's remedies are specific: make service information and basic diagnostic software free, and cap manufacturer software licence fees at $100 a month or $1,000 a year per brand. The figures are the group's own estimates, built from its survey and price checks, and no automaker response is recorded in the report. The study lands as right-to-repair bills move through US state legislatures, and it gives those campaigns a single cost number to cite.
