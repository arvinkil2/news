**Latest (Saturday, October 10):** The ruling has a name, a judge and a sharper mechanism. In an October 8 order in *Freestyle World, Inc. v. United States*, U.S. Court of International Trade Judge Richard K. Eaton certified an injunctive class under the trade court's Rule 23(b)(2), rejecting the government's argument that differing refund amounts make the claims too individualized for class treatment. The certified class covers all importers of record who paid duties under the challenged tariff orders between February 1, 2025 and February 19, 2026, excluding those that already filed their own recovery suits, according to Bloomberg reporting confirmed by trade-press accounts.

Eaton's decision opens a path to an eventual court order requiring Customs and Border Protection to liquidate or reliquidate the entries of every remaining class member and refund the difference, rather than leaving each importer to navigate the CAPE refund portal alone. Bloomberg reports Customs has already fully processed almost $130 billion in refunds since the Supreme Court struck the tariffs down, with filings putting the CAPE-processed figure between roughly $126 and $136 billion. Eaton wrote that about $30 billion in unlawfully collected duties would remain on deposit in the Treasury if the administrative process stopped there, and said the importers who never file through CAPE are believed to be overwhelmingly small businesses that lack the resources, awareness or both. The certification does not pay anyone yet; the trade court has a closed conference set for October 13 and scheduling filings due October 22, and the Justice Department continues to contest refund jurisdiction. Status remains litigated.

The U.S. Court of International Trade will allow importers to pursue a class-action lawsuit over the now-defunct tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, potentially extending refunds far beyond the companies that filed their own suits. In a slip order issued Thursday, the court certified a class of importers who paid IEEPA tariffs between February 1, 2025, and February 19, 2026, excluding those already pursuing separate lawsuits; more than 4,000 individual suits have been filed so far.

The Supreme Court ruled the IEEPA tariffs illegal in February, and the trade court ordered Customs and Border Protection to refund roughly $166 billion collected under them. CBP has since paid out over $100 billion through its CAPE refund portal, but the court found that smaller importers are falling through the cracks: many lack the resources or awareness to file individual claims, and without a class mechanism about $30 billion in unlawful duties would remain with the government.

The certification does not immediately pay anyone. The court set a closed conference for October 13 and an October 22 deadline for further scheduling filings, while the Justice Department is still appealing the refund order on jurisdictional grounds. The class certification creates a single path for non-litigants toward a court-ordered refund of their entries.
