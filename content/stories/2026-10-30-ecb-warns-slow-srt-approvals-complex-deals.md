Banks looking to offload credit risk through significant risk transfers may have to wait longer for the supervisor's blessing if the deals are complex. Bloomberg reported on October 30 that the European Central Bank is stepping up scrutiny of SRT trades backed by insurers, warning lenders that structures it cannot see through clearly face slower approvals.

The market context is a boom. SRTs let a bank transfer the credit risk on a pool of loans to investors, freeing capital against those loans without selling them. As competition for the high yielding instruments has heated up, hedge funds and other buyers have turned to insurers to provide guarantees that cut the cost of bank loans they sometimes use to finance the trades. The ECB, Bloomberg reported, is alert to the risk that such arrangements create or amplify linkages within the banking system, though it has so far seen no evidence of the problem materialising.

The supervisory concern is circularity. If the protection on a bank's loan book is ultimately guaranteed inside the financial system, and financed with leverage provided by banks themselves, the risk has not left the system; it has changed its address and become harder to map. That is the same interconnectedness question the ECB has been raising about private credit and non-bank finance more broadly, now applied to a specific and rapidly growing instrument.

No figures for deal volumes or approval timelines are stated here because the underlying report is single sourced and paywalled beyond its opening paragraphs. The direction, however, is consistent with the ECB's published posture this year of fast tracking simple, standardised capital operations while reserving detailed review for complex ones. For bank treasurers, the practical message is that capital planning built on quick SRT execution carries a new timing risk, and for the rates market it is a reminder that euro area credit supply runs partly through a supervisory queue in Frankfurt.
