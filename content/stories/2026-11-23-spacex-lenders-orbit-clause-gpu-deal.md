Lenders backing SpaceX's $43 billion GPU financing, known as Project Saturn, tried to bar the Nvidia chips securing the debt from being launched into space for orbital data centres, according to Financial Times reporting citing people familiar with the talks. Their concern is recoverability: chips installed in a data centre can be repossessed and resold after a default, while chips in orbit effectively cannot. Whether the provision made it into the final agreements is not clear. SpaceX says the chips in this arrangement are intended for use on Earth, and Moody's says they will sit in US data centres majority-owned, controlled and operated by SpaceX.

The financing builds on the binding commitment SpaceX announced in October for an approximately $40 billion equipment lease with an Apollo-managed entity, covering Nvidia Vera Rubin systems with deliveries expected to begin in the first half of 2027. More than $30 billion of debt was sold to large investors on Thursday, November 19. The largest piece is a $25 billion investment-grade bond backed by about five years of lease payments for the GPUs, priced at about 3.45 percentage points over Treasuries for a yield of roughly 8.3 percent, against about 7.2 percent on SpaceX debt maturing in 2031. The bonds are issued through a special purpose vehicle, mature in 2032 and repay principal in under four years. Moody's rated the bond A3, one notch above SpaceX itself, and KBRA rated it A-minus, citing hell-or-high-water lease clauses under which payments are owed regardless of circumstances. Apollo and Goldman Sachs led the deal, with anchor commitments reported from BlackRock, Goldman Sachs Asset Management, KKR and Pimco.

The tension is structural. Elon Musk has promoted orbital data centres as a way around land and power constraints on Earth, which is precisely the use that would put the collateral beyond a receiver's reach, and fast chip depreciation makes physical access worth more, not less, to lenders.
