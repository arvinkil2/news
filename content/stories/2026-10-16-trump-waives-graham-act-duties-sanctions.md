**Latest (Sunday afternoon):** Former vice president Mike Pence posted on X on Sunday that waiving the Graham Act's sanctions dishonors the legacy of the late Senator Lindsey Graham, the Act's author, is not in the national interest and should be reversed, The Hill reported. In a second post, Pence wrote: "Putin is a war criminal and only understands strength."

**Latest (Sunday):** Energy Secretary Chris Wright defended the waiver on ABC's This Week on Sunday, the day the Graham Act's provisions had been due to take effect, The Hill reported. A short RIA Novosti report of his Sunday television remarks quoted Wright saying the United States had eased zero sanctions on Russian oil. In the same CNN interview, as reported by TASS, Wright said Washington is in contact with Beijing on applying the Act, that the Act's duties are aimed not at Russia itself but at countries that trade with Russia, naming China and India as the two important buyers, and that the discussions with China cover the conflict and long term United States China trade relations.

**Latest (Saturday):** Secretary of State Marco Rubio defended the waiver when a reporter put Senator Ted Cruz's criticism to him, that the move was a big mistake that only serves to benefit President Vladimir Putin. Rubio said the measures were not sanctions on Russia alone: the Act also carries tariffs on a number of countries, India for example, and the president wants to take the 60-day period to analyze what imposing both the sanctions and the tariffs would mean for the global economy and for the United States economy. He said one reason the waivers were written into the bill was the understanding that a period of time would be needed to study this, given the pressure on global energy supplies from Iran and the Strait of Hormuz, from the Houthis attacking Saudi Arabia, and from the Russia-Ukraine war, in which energy infrastructure is being hit. India, he said, has voluntarily diversified its energy portfolio but still takes a substantial amount of energy from Russia, because a pivot cannot happen from one day to the next, and it is also an important ally and partner of the United States. On the diesel deal, Rubio said the diesel Putin is going to sell would reach the global market one way or the other, and the only difference was how much of it the United States was going to get. He added that the administration had also issued a 60-day waiver on Russian oil a few months earlier, when Hormuz was being challenged, so the step was not unprecedented.

**Latest (Saturday 18:44 ET):** Senator Darline Graham of South Carolina, who was appointed to her late brother Lindsey Graham's seat and helped ensure the Act's passage, broke publicly with the president at a campaign stop on Saturday. She said she and the president share the same goal, ending the war and getting fuel prices down, and that the sanctions bill is one of the tools he can use. She added that she would personally choose to keep the pressure on Russia, because she does not trust President Vladimir Putin. Senator Rick Scott of Florida told the Associated Press that Russia is the aggressor and the waiver is not the first thing he would be doing. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said the waiver gives the Russian economy life support, and Senator Elizabeth Warren said the president is thumbing his nose at Congress and urged her colleagues to take action to reverse course. Representative Gregory Meeks of New York, the ranking Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, introduced legislation on Friday that would block the administration's diesel deal with Moscow, and said on Saturday that Congress is not helpless and must act. No quick reversal is available in practice: the House and Senate are on recess until after the November 3 midterm elections, and the Associated Press reported it is unlikely Congress would be in a position to challenge the waiver until lawmakers return to Washington.

**Latest (Saturday 10:44 ET):** The waiver drew public criticism from Republicans in Congress on Saturday. Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina called the move “beyond irresponsible” in a post on X, arguing that the administration was failing to enforce a law the Senate passed 86 to 11 and was capitulating to Russia. Senator John Cornyn of Texas posted the news on Saturday morning with the comment “Lindsey would be appalled,” referring to the late Senator Lindsey Graham, the Act's author. Representative Don Bacon of Nebraska wrote that the president is prolonging the war, not ending it. Democrats attacked the waiver in similar terms: Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut, who helped pass the bill, called it a “craven, cowardly action,” and Representative Don Beyer of Virginia said it betrayed Ukrainian partners and defied congressional intent. No vote, override effort or other congressional next step was reported.

**Saturday 07:42 ET:** President Volodymyr Zelenskyy attacked the waiver directly on Saturday, saying the decision came the same day Russia struck 15 critical power generation facilities in one of the largest attacks on Ukraine's grid, and that a United States sanctions blackout, easing pressure on the aggressor, amounts to financial rewards for President Vladimir Putin and encourages him to keep attacking. He said the Graham Act could have imposed effective pressure to end the war and still can, and warned that Russia is trying to leave millions of Ukrainians without electricity and heating before winter. On the Russian side, Putin envoy Kirill Dmitriev called the suspension of the Graham Act an important and truly historic moment, and said the Act would have damaged the United States economy and global markets first. The two reactions fix the political reading of the order on both sides: Kyiv treats the waiver as relief granted to Moscow at the moment of a grid campaign, and Moscow's own envoy treats it as a win worth celebrating in public.

President Donald Trump has signed an executive order implementing the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, the sanctions law he signed on September 18, and has used it to waive the Act's duties and sanctions provisions before they take effect.

The order first sets the rates the Act provides for. The duty on goods from Russia rises by 500 percent ad valorem. The duty on goods from the five largest importers of Russian crude oil and natural gas rises by 100 percent if a country knowingly makes new purchases of Russian crude or gas on or after October 18, 2026, and the duty on goods from the top five facilitators of Russian oil sanctions evasion rises by 100 percent. Each increase stacks on top of any other duty already in force.

The next operative section then removes them. Under section 115 of the Act, the order waives the application of any duties provided for in sections 112 and 113 of the Act or in the order itself. It also waives the sanctions provision in section 102(d), and the sanctions provisions and restrictions in sections 102(a), 102(e) and 103 through 111 and 117, covering the Act's measures against foreign persons. The order states that the president has submitted to Congress a certification that the waivers are in the national interests of the United States, with a report explaining the basis, as the Act requires. The waivers remain in effect until the president, or the secretary of state in consultation with the Treasury and the Trade Representative, determines that further action is appropriate.

One limit is stated expressly. Nothing in the order affects sanctions or other measures under the earlier executive orders addressing Russia's harmful foreign activities, continued on March 24, 2026, including every person already sanctioned under them. The pre-existing sanctions regime therefore stays in place. What is suspended is the new layer Congress added in September.

In place of the duties, the order sets up monitoring. The United States Trade Representative, consulting the secretaries of state, treasury and energy, is to watch imports and purchases of Russian crude oil and natural gas, track countries that facilitate Russian oil sanctions evasion, report regularly to the president, and promptly flag any circumstance that might indicate a need for further action, including with respect to the waivers.

The Act passed the Senate 86 to 11 on August 7 and the House 262 to 159 on September 16, and its October 18 deadline had become the next test of the administration's Russia energy policy. Twelve senators wrote to the president on October 13 urging him to rescind the October 9 diesel deal with Moscow and to use the Graham Act's pressure tools instead. European leaders meeting in Brussels on October 15 and 16 criticised the diesel arrangement, and Britain tightened its own sanctions on Russian oil on October 16. The waiver resolves the October 18 question in the opposite direction: the tariff threat over the largest buyers of Russian energy is now held in reserve, revocable at Washington's discretion, rather than imposed by statute on a fixed date.
