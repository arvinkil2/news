**Latest (Monday, Jakarta):** Indonesian officials plan to meet MSCI executives on Monday to seek clarification on the proposed reclassification, Bloomberg reported, citing people familiar with the matter. The Financial Services Authority and the Indonesia Stock Exchange want to know why MSCI put standalone status on the table and launched its consultation ahead of a review previously expected in November, and the Indonesian side said it was blindsided by a threat more severe than what it thought months of talks had covered, Bloomberg reported. No outcome of any meeting had been reported in the reporting opened.

**Latest (Monday, Jakarta):** The sell-off resumed when trading reopened on Monday. CNBC reported the benchmark index plunged more than 5 percent to its lowest level in six years, the lowest since early October 2020, extending Friday's crash as concern over the proposed reclassification kept driving foreign investors out.

The exchange tightened its trading limits from Monday. The Indonesia Stock Exchange cut the lower auto rejection limit, the most a share can fall in a day, from 15 percent to 7 percent for shares priced above Rp10, effective from October 19 to December 31, 2026, under Board of Directors Decree Kep-00157/BEI/10-2026, which replaces Decree Kep-00136/BEI/09-2026. The upper limits were left unchanged, and the step has the support of the Financial Services Authority. Corporate secretary Elsierra Putri Yosita said in the exchange's statement, reported by Antara, that the change is meant to keep trading orderly, fair and efficient, to guard against volatility, and to give investors time to evaluate the information available against the fundamentals of the Indonesian economy. The exchange will re-evaluate the limit in light of market developments after December 31.

Goldman Sachs set out the cost of a full removal in a Sunday note reported by CNBC. The bank estimated about $5.6 billion in passive fund outflows if Indonesia leaves MSCI's emerging market indexes, equal to 18 days of average daily trading volume for constituents of the MSCI Indonesia Investable Market Index, with a further $2.7 billion at risk if FTSE Russell later takes similar action, which the bank stressed is a separate scenario. Active funds benchmarked to the two index providers hold more than $8 billion in Indonesian equities, and any selling by them would be discretionary. Goldman put foreign net selling at $5.1 billion this year, foreign ownership down to 25 percent in October from 27 percent in January, and the value of foreign holdings down to about $130 billion from $250 billion. It kept an underweight rating, put the MSCI Indonesia Index down 47 percent this year with a forward price to earnings ratio of 7.3 times, the lowest since the global financial crisis, and expected Bank Indonesia to raise rates by 25 basis points in each of the fourth quarter of 2026 and the first quarter of 2027. Reuters, in its report on the consultation and Friday's session, put UBS's estimate of passive outflows at about $4 billion, with up to $15 billion more potentially from active investors.

On Friday the fall deepened well past the open. Investing.com put the Jakarta Composite at 5,254.53, down 10.4 percent, later in the session, and said the intraday plunge exceeded 11 percent, the steepest one day fall since January 1998, even as other Asian markets rebounded. That takes the index below the 5,300 level Kiwoom Sekuritas had flagged as the next exposed area after a break of 5,700.

Indonesia's stock market opened in a crash on Friday after MSCI said the country no longer merits emerging market status. The Jakarta Composite Index fell 469.86 points, or 8.01 percent, to 5,393.92 at the open, and the Indonesia Stock Exchange halted trading at 9:00 a.m. Jakarta time when the fall crossed its 8 percent threshold. The blue chip LQ45 index fell 8.33 percent to 540.95. Trading was set to resume after 30 minutes under rules adopted in April 2025, which impose a further 30 minute halt beyond a 15 percent fall and a suspension beyond 20 percent. The rupiah weakened 0.46 percent to 18,021 per dollar at the open, from a previous close of 17,938.

The trigger was MSCI's announcement, made on Thursday in New York, that it has opened a consultation on reclassifying the MSCI Indonesia indexes from emerging market to standalone market status across its global and regional composite indexes. Standalone is the designation MSCI reserves for markets it judges to meet neither emerging nor frontier criteria, and MSCI said a frontier downgrade would not adequately reflect the severity of Indonesia's problems. It expects to proceed unless the consultation produces clear evidence of material improvement in market governance and functioning. Feedback is due by April 30, 2027, and a decision is expected on or before June 30, 2027.

MSCI said market participants had raised significant investability concerns in its June 2026 classification review, centred on persistent opacity in shareholding structures and suspected coordinated trading, and that reforms announced since then have not been implemented consistently or backed by meaningful enforcement. The reforms include disclosure of shareholdings above 1 percent, more detailed investor classification, reporting of high shareholding concentrations, and a roadmap toward a 15 percent minimum free float. A freeze MSCI imposed in January remains in force until further notice: no increases to foreign inclusion factors or share counts, no additions to the Indonesia investable market index, and no upward moves between size segments.

The exchange pushed back before the open. Jeffrey Hendrik, president director of the Indonesia Stock Exchange, said the bourse respects MSCI's independence but would seek clarification on the basis for the consultation, pointing to concrete steps taken with the Financial Services Authority to improve transparency and market integrity that he said had drawn positive responses from global investors. He stressed that the announcement opens a consultation rather than delivering a final decision.

The fall extends the worst year among the world's major markets. Indonesian equities are down more than 30 percent in 2026, foreign investors have sold a net $4.7 billion, and Moody's and Fitch have both moved the sovereign outlook to negative. Angus Mackintosh, an ASEAN specialist at Aletheia Capital in Singapore, called the MSCI move heavy handed but a wake up call, arguing the core problem is a failure to sanction those involved in stock manipulation. Liza Camelia Suryanata, head of research at Kiwoom Sekuritas Indonesia, warned that a break below the 5,700 area would leave the index exposed toward 5,300, and that reclassification would risk further foreign outflows and volatility.
