**Latest.** A US jury convicted Huawei Technologies on Tuesday of conspiracy to steal trade secrets and of bank fraud, the Associated Press and Reuters reported, after a trial of more than two months in New York in which prosecutors called more than 60 witnesses and the defence called 15. Jurors acquitted Huawei of racketeering conspiracy, the most serious charge it faced, under which prosecutors had accused the company of operating as a criminal enterprise to steal intellectual property from American companies. Huawei and three affiliates faced 12 counts in all, including wire fraud, sanctions violations and obstruction of justice. Huawei attorney Brian Heberlig said the company would appeal the convictions but called the racketeering acquittal a vindication, saying that count was the only one that brought all of the allegations together and that the jury's rejection of it repudiated the central government narrative in the case. Prosecutors had argued that Huawei misled HSBC, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas about its business in Iran and stole technology from companies including Cisco Systems, T-Mobile, Quintel and Fujitsu. The verdict's effect on Huawei's business is unclear: the company has long been barred from selling equipment to US carriers and is blacklisted by several Western allies, while expanding in chipmaking as an alternative supplier in China to US chipmakers.

A federal jury in Brooklyn began deliberating on Wednesday, November 18, on whether Huawei Technologies and three affiliated companies ran a 20-year criminal conspiracy to steal technology from American companies, mislead banks about the company's business in Iran and obstruct federal investigations. The defendants face 12 criminal counts, including racketeering conspiracy under the RICO statute, bank fraud, wire fraud, conspiracy to steal trade secrets and obstruction of justice, in a trial that heard more than 60 prosecution witnesses and about 15 defence witnesses before US District Judge Ann Donnelly.

Prosecutors told jurors that Huawei encouraged employees to obtain confidential technology from competitors including Cisco and T-Mobile and concealed its control of Skycom, its Iran-facing business, from HSBC and other banks. The defence argued that the government began with guilt and worked backwards, that the banks knew about the Iranian operations, and that Huawei's success came from engineering and investment rather than theft. Jurors were scheduled to resume deliberations on Thursday.
