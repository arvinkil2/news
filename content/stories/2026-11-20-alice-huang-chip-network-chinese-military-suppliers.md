**Latest (November 25):** Companies linked to Alice Huang's family and associates raised financing against up to 764 AI servers and modules valued at $270 million, plus $31 million in networking equipment, according to filings in the People's Bank of China's credit registry reviewed by Reuters. Century Litong, an Nvidia preferred cloud partner connected through corporate records to Ms. Huang's sister Huang Yu and her husband Kang Wei, accounted for five leasing arrangements covering up to 512 servers with a declared value of 1.42 billion yuan ($200 million) between May and December 2024, four of them listing equipment at the Yangtze River Delta AI supercomputing centre in Shanghai; a separate deal for 128 servers worth $57 million was financed by an arm of AVIC, the state-backed aviation and defence conglomerate. Tencent, a roughly 10 percent shareholder in the centre's developer Century Huatong, said it never owned or operated the centre and has never had a commercial relationship with Megaspeed. Reuters states the records do not establish that the servers contained chips prohibited from export to China, or how and when the equipment entered the country.

Companies linked to Alice Huang, the woman at the center of the Nvidia chip scandal, sit inside a network of Chinese technology firms and state-owned defense contractors that supply the People's Liberation Army, according to a New York Times investigation drawing on a confidential analysis by Strider Technologies, an independent intelligence firm. The analysis shows about $3.6 billion in Chinese government money flowed through companies associated with Ms. Huang's family or associates, funding technology projects including data centers that received Nvidia chips, with secondary summaries of the investigation tracing part of the supply chain through Singapore-based Megaspeed and Alibaba-backed Zhejiang Lab and naming missile, radar and military aircraft programs among the contractors' work.

The Times describes most of the relationships as indirect and says the vital question of whether Nvidia chips ended up being used to support Chinese military projects remains murky. Nvidia has previously said it found no evidence of illicit chip transfers, and no official record has confirmed the military-supplier link.
