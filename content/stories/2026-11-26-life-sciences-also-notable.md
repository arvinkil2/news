Engineered gloves cut bacterial transfer in laboratory tests. Researchers at the University of Toronto coated standard nitrile gloves with a thin rubber layer moulded with microposts and grooves, designed to stop microbes sticking rather than to kill them. In motorised touch tests using cultures of five pathogenic bacteria and one pathogenic fungus, the coated gloves reduced the number of cells transferred by an average of 95 to 99 percent compared with uncoated gloves, for both pickup and onward spread. The study, led by Desmond van den Berg with senior author Ben Hatton, was published in Science Translational Medicine. The team is testing the gloves with clinicians at North York General Hospital, and the work remains a proof of concept with no commercial scale or routine clinical-use evidence yet.

MHRA flags a Takeda Prostap labelling defect. The MHRA's Class 4 defect notification EL(26)A/52, published November 25, covers two batches of Takeda Prostap PD DCS 1.88 mg (leuprorelin acetate), batch 523373 and the now-expired batch 518961. Step 5 of the administration instructions and the syringe label incorrectly state the product can be given subcutaneously or intramuscularly; the approved route is subcutaneous only. The MHRA said no manufacturing, formulation or purity defect was identified, no confirmed cases of administration by the wrong route or associated harm were identified, and expected harm severity is minor, mainly temporary injection-site reactions. Affected batches will continue to be distributed until corrected replacement stock arrives, currently anticipated in March or April 2027, and paediatric endocrinology teams were asked to review administration records for any intramuscular use.
