Nigeria's government has put a date on the end of its high-rate era. Finance Minister Taiwo Oyedele told the Nigerian Economic Summit in Abuja on Monday that the government wants inflation brought to low single digits before the end of next year, meaning by the end of 2027, so that it can create the conditions for single digit interest rates.

The arithmetic he offered is the case for urgency. The economy is growing at 4.43 percent while borrowing costs sit close to 30 percent. Interest, he said, is the second biggest item on the government's own profit and loss, the price paid for stability, and the same spread caps private credit: households saving for decades to buy a car outright, and small businesses priced out of loans the government has had to subsidise directly. His causal chain was fiscal as much as monetary: unpaid taxes force the state to print money to spend, which shows up as inflation, which forces the monetary authority to raise rates to fight it.

The target is a statement of intent, not a policy instrument, and the gap it has to close is large. Nothing in the remarks changes the Central Bank of Nigeria's stance today, and the summit panel around the minister underlined the distance still to travel: Anambra State Governor Charles Soludo argued growth needs to run at 7 to 12 percent for a decade to cut poverty materially, against roughly 4 percent now. What the remarks do change is accountability. A named minister, a named summit and a named date, the end of 2027, give Nigeria's disinflation programme a public deadline against which every monthly inflation print from here will be read.
