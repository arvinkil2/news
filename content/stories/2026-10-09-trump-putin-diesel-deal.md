**Latest:** OFAC published Russia-related General License 135 on Friday, "Authorizing Transactions Related to the Sale, Delivery, Offloading, and Importation of Diesel Fuel of Russian Federation Origin" — the sanctions mechanism that makes the Trump-Putin diesel announcement executable. This is the first concrete paperwork behind the pivot.

President Trump announced Friday that he had concluded what he called a highly successful discussion with Russian President Vladimir Putin, with an agreement that Russia will immediately supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel fuel to American and global markets, followed by another 500,000 tons in November and 1,000,000 tons immediately after that. Politico reported that Trump is lifting sanctions on Russian diesel and suggested the United States itself may buy, framing the move around surging fuel prices.

The announcement caps a week of escalating diesel brinkmanship. On October 1, Putin told the Valdai Forum that Russia would not supply diesel to global markets until sanctions against Moscow are lifted, and Moscow extended its diesel export ban through the end of October. Trump had spent weeks blaming Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries for the global diesel shortage and pressing Kyiv to stop them. US diesel prices have set records above $6.50 a gallon, with the midterm elections weeks away, and The Hill reported earlier Friday that Trump was teasing a diesel announcement.

The pivot lands in a market already stretched on every side. The Hormuz tanker war is locking Gulf diesel inside the region, China suspended product exports for October, and the G7 and IEA moved last week on a 100-million-barrel coordinated stock release. Against that backdrop, Russian barrels are the fastest available relief, and the White House is betting voters will reward lower pump prices more than they punish the optics of buying fuel from Moscow.

The risks are political, not logistical. The Atlantic reported this week that Trump's envoy John Coale has been developing a sanctions-relief framework for Russia, and analysts expect sharp pushback from Kyiv, European capitals, and sanctions hawks in Congress, who will argue the deal funnels revenue to Russia's war machine. How the diesel is priced, who carries it, and which sanctions are actually waived remain undisclosed, and those details will determine whether this is a one-off pressure valve or the start of a broader US-Russia economic reengagement.
