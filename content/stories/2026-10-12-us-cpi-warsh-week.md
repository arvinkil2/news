**Latest (14:42 ET):** The Beige Book, the Fed's district survey prepared for the October 27 to 28 FOMC, pointed the same way as the morning's CPI, only broader. Nine of the 12 districts reported growth, two moderate, three modest and four slight, with three reporting no change, down from 10 growing districts in August, and the national summary dropped its headline characterisation of activity altogether, noting that reports outside manufacturing indicated little growth overall. Manufacturing carried the expansion, led by Dallas and by data centre, power generation and defence demand, while Philadelphia and New York slowed to slight growth and Chicago went flat. Consumer spending was little or unchanged in most districts and, in more than half, was being driven by high income households, with strain spreading to middle income families in San Francisco's report and Cleveland nonprofits reporting a record share of clients whose finances deteriorated. Employment was little changed overall. Prices were the shift: three districts reported robust price growth, eight moderate and one modest, and four said the pace of increases had picked up, against one district on each count in August. Higher oil prices appeared in every district report, diesel most often, and firms expected further increases as hedges and fixed contracts expire and reprice, with Philadelphia's future price indexes among their highest readings in 35 years. Consumer facing firms, the survey found, still cannot pass costs through, so the pressure is landing on margins. The outlook stayed positive, but for slight growth, weighted toward manufacturing.

**Latest (13:49 ET):** The unrounded detail made the print look firmer still. Core prices rose 0.335 percent on the month, close to rounding to 0.4 percent, with core goods up 0.3 percent against 0.11 percent in August and supercore inflation at 3.13 percent on the year, up from 3.02 percent. More than 52 percent of items rose at better than a 3 percent pace in September, according to XTB's read of the release. At the US open the S&P 500 fell 0.8 percent and the Nasdaq 100 fell 1 percent, the 10-year yield briefly touched 5.33 percent before settling near 5.27 percent, the two-year eased to about 4.86 percent after spiking to 4.95 percent, gold fell about 2 percent toward $4,075 an ounce and silver dropped about 6 percent to $55.60. Market pricing for an October hike sits between 45 and 50 percent across trackers, up from about 21 percent before the data. The Federal Reserve Beige Book, at 14:00 ET, is the next release.

**Earlier (11:17 ET):** The repricing extended through the morning. The Bloomberg dollar index rose 0.4 percent to its highest since May 2025, the euro fell 0.6 percent to $1.1133 and the dollar bought 158.85 yen, while gold fell 1.4 percent to $4,102 an ounce, a tightening scare pattern rather than a flight to safety. Brent held near $106 with WTI up 1.5 percent at $95.57. The Federal Reserve Beige Book, at 14:00 ET, is the next test of whether the move holds.

**Earlier (08:57 ET):** September CPI came in hotter than expected where it matters. Headline prices rose 0.6 percent on the month, matching consensus and up from 0.4 percent in August, lifting the annual rate to 3.7 percent against a 3.6 percent forecast and 3.4 percent in August. Core prices, excluding food and energy, rose 0.3 percent on the month, matching August and beating the 0.2 percent forecast, and 2.6 percent on the year, up from 2.4 percent and above the 2.5 percent expected. Energy did the heavy lifting in the headline: the energy index rose 5.2 percent on the month and 20.4 percent on the year, and the BLS gasoline index jumped 9.1 percent on the month, accounting for more than half of the monthly all items increase, with fuel oil up 15.3 percent. Food prices were flat on the month and up 2.5 percent on the year. Inside the core, shelter accelerated to 0.4 percent on the month from 0.3 percent in August, services excluding energy rose 0.4 percent, and recreation, used cars and trucks, education, airline fares and apparel all increased, while communication fell.

The market reaction was immediate. Two-year Treasury yields climbed 12 basis points and the 10-year rose 7 basis points in the first minutes after the release, stock futures turned negative (Dow down 0.16 percent, S&P 500 down 0.24 percent and Nasdaq 100 down 0.49 percent at 08:33 ET), and market odds on a rate hike at the October 27 to 28 meeting jumped to 45 percent on LSEG pricing, from 20 percent before the data, with one market tracker putting the move nearer 50 percent. Natixis economist Christopher Hodge, quoted in market coverage of the print, said the numbers were far enough from consensus to make October a debate and to give the hawks more ammunition, while his base case remains that the Fed does not move this month, given how Williams and Jefferson have argued there is no rush to tighten again. Bowman made the same argument hours before the release, saying she sees no urgent need for further action.

Going in, the Wall Street Journal poll had expected headline inflation of 3.6 percent year on year, up from 3.4 percent in August, on a 0.6 percent monthly rise, and core inflation of 2.5 percent, up from 2.4 percent, on a slower 0.2 percent monthly rise. Markets had settled on a pause this month: LSEG put the probability of no change on October 28 at 78 percent on Wednesday morning, while a December hike remained the baseline in strategist notes and three hikes in total were priced over the next 12 months. The week's Fed commentary pointed both ways: New York's John Williams backed one more hike this year but saw none needed in 2027, Boston's Susan Collins expected rates to rise a bit further this year, and Cleveland's Beth Hammack has argued for a quicker move to restrictive policy.

What follows now decides whether the repricing holds. The Federal Reserve Beige Book is released at 14:00 ET today. Thursday brings September PPI, retail sales and jobless claims, and Chair Kevin Warsh speaks later this week at the IMF annual meetings, his last scheduled appearance before the October 17 blackout ahead of the October 27 to 28 FOMC. A core reading at 0.4 percent would have forced a fuller revisit of the terminal rate; at 0.3 percent, the argument is narrower and now runs through Warsh's remarks and the PPI composition.
