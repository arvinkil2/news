**Latest.** France has identified Iran as the origin of the attacks, Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said at a press briefing during a G7 summit in Paris on Monday, naming the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and its affiliated entities Shahid Kaveh and CyberAv3ngers, The Guardian reported. Barrot said he would raise the issue at a meeting of EU foreign ministers the same day and ask the EU to designate CyberAv3ngers and Shahid Kaveh under its sanctions regime for cyber-attacks against the European Union, and to designate the IRGC as a terrorist organisation; France will also summon the Iranian ambassador to Paris. Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau said her office had opened an investigation, and Vincent Strubel, head of Anssi, France's national cybersecurity agency, said Iran was the second most aggressive source of cyber-attacks in France after Russia. The Guardian's report presented the attribution for the October attack on as many as 700 French supermarkets and shops, in which temperatures rose to as much as 50 degrees Celsius and thousands of tonnes of food were dumped, and noted similar incidents since in other European countries; the retailers were said to use refrigeration software from the Italian company Epta. No Iranian response was reported.

**Earlier.** Dozens of supermarkets in Belgium, Luxembourg and Sweden found their refrigeration and freezer temperatures raised overnight in a suspected cyberattack, the BBC and The Guardian reported on Monday. Delhaize told Belgian media a computer incident had affected about 40 of its stores in Belgium and Luxembourg, Carrefour said 15 of its Belgian stores were affected but stayed open, and in Sweden several ICA stores and a small number of Coop stores were hit, with one ICA store in Sjobo reporting its freezers had been set to 50 degrees Celsius. Millions of euros of food had to be thrown away according to local media, including about 100,000 euros of produce at one Delhaize branch in Hoeilaart, and the Belgian and Swedish national cybersecurity agencies said they were monitoring the incident. No one has been identified as responsible, and the attack follows an October incident in France in which hackers took control of cooling units at up to 700 supermarkets, pushing temperatures from as low as minus 27 degrees to well over 50 degrees and causing millions of euros of food losses.
