Ghana came home from the Russia-Africa Summit in Moscow with a list. Foreign Minister Samuel Okudzeto Ablakwa said on Saturday that President John Mahama's direct talks with Vladimir Putin on Friday produced six commitments: Russian help to establish a 2,000 megawatt nuclear power plant, a new defence pact to acquire strategic military assets, deeper agricultural cooperation on grain production and fertiliser supply, more Russian scholarships for Ghanaian students, a revival of the two countries' Permanent Joint Commission for Cooperation with Moscow hosting within weeks, and a declaration by Putin that Ghanaians are no longer allowed to participate in the war in Ukraine in any circumstances.

The nuclear pledge is the headline and the least specified. Ablakwa framed it as reviving Kwame Nkrumah's nuclear ambition, cut short by the 1966 coup, and as a guarantee of energy security for industrialisation. No timeline, financing structure or technology provider was announced, and Ghana has pursued nuclear power with several partners over the years without breaking ground. The commitment is real as a diplomatic fact; as a power project it is at the statement stage.

The recruitment pledge may matter sooner. Mahama raised the recruitment of Ghanaian nationals to fight for Russia in Ukraine, and Ablakwa said Putin declared the practice over, whether through irregular recruitment or voluntary enlistment. Ghana ranks second among African nations for students in Russia, which is the channel through which some recruitment has reportedly flowed, and the scholarship increase sits alongside the ban in the same package.

Status: announced. The test is documentary: a signed nuclear cooperation agreement, a defence pact text and the joint commission meeting in Moscow would convert a summit list into a programme. Until then this is the clearest statement yet of how far Accra is willing to balance its Western partnerships with a Russian track.
