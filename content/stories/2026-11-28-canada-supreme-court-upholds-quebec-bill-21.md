Canada's Supreme Court on Friday largely upheld Quebec's secularism law, Bill 21, in a landmark ruling on how far legislatures can go in overriding Charter rights. The 2019 law prohibits public servants in positions of authority, including teachers, police officers, judges and government lawyers, from wearing religious symbols such as hijabs, turbans and yarmulkes at work.

The court confirmed that Quebec validly used section 33 of the Charter, the notwithstanding clause, to shield the law, and that the clause can be used pre-emptively, before any court challenge. Chief Justice Richard Wagner's lead reasons described the law as rooted in Quebec's legitimate and historical commitment to ensuring the laicity of the state in a free and democratic society. Six of the seven justices agreed on the validity of the clause's use; all seven agreed the court could still declare a law inconsistent with Charter rights even when the clause is invoked, though the majority made no such declaration here.

One provision did not survive. All seven justices found that section 8, insofar as it required members of Quebec's National Assembly to uncover their faces, infringed democratic rights under section 3 of the Charter, which the notwithstanding clause cannot override. Three justices, O'Bonsawin, Karakatsanis and Martin, wrote that the law also violates freedom of religion, expression and gender equality, but the majority held that the gender equality guarantee in section 28 could not be used to strike the law down.

Reaction split along familiar lines. Rights groups warned the ruling unfairly targets Muslim women and weakens protections for fundamental rights, while Alberta's premier said the decision confirms the notwithstanding clause is a legitimate and essential part of the Constitution. The judgment settles Bill 21's legal status; the political argument over pre-emptive use of section 33, which must be renewed every five years, continues. Status: judgment delivered November 27, 2026; law remains in force with section 8 narrowed.
