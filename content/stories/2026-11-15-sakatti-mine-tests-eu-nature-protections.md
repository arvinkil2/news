Anglo American's proposed Sakatti mine in northern Finland has become a test case for European nature law. Parts of the copper, nickel and cobalt deposit lie beneath the Viiankiaapa wetland, a protected mire about 150 kilometres north of the Arctic Circle. Finland's government has requested the European Commission's opinion before deciding whether to grant an exemption from Natura 2000 protection under the EU Habitats Directive.

Sakatti carries weight beyond one project because it has been designated a strategic project under the EU Critical Raw Materials Act, the law meant to speed up European supply of the metals used in electric vehicles, wind turbines, defence equipment and data centres. Anglo American has explored the site since 2004 and plans to start extraction in the early 2030s. The project is at the permitting stage, and the exemption is the decision that blocks or unlocks the land use plan.

The conflict is over compensation. Anglo American proposes to offset the mine's impact by restoring degraded habitats nearby. Finnish forestry and permitting authorities consider the proposed measures insufficient to compensate for the damage, according to Politico's reporting, and the Finnish Association for Nature Conservation warns that an exemption could open the way for further mining projects in protected areas. Viiankiaapa is also covered by Finnish mire conservation provisions, which would need to be amended by parliament for the mine to proceed.

The Commission's assessment is ongoing and no date has been announced for its opinion. If the Finnish government grants the derogation, the decision is expected to be challenged in the Supreme Administrative Court. For the mining sector, the outcome matters as a precedent: it will show whether strategic project status under the Critical Raw Materials Act can carry a mine through a Natura 2000 conflict, or whether habitat law remains a hard stop even for designated projects.
