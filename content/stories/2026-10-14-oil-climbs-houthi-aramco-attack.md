**Latest (14:47 ET):** A container ship has sunk in the Strait of Hormuz. Singapore's Maritime and Port Authority said the Singapore flagged GFS Pride was struck by an unknown projectile while transiting the strait on Tuesday, caught fire and sank; all 21 crew were evacuated and no injuries were reported. Pakistan's Hydrographer warned mariners the ship went down about 4 nautical miles north west of Ras al-Sheikh Masud, on Oman's Musandam Peninsula. A second Singapore flagged container ship, GFS Giselle, was hit by a projectile on Wednesday, took hull damage and reached a safe anchorage with all 22 crew aboard. Iran's Revolutionary Guards gave a different account, saying a ship on an unauthorised route hit naval mines and sank; that is Tehran's claim and is not corroborated by the Singapore authority, which attributes the sinking to a projectile strike. Reporting differs on a possible second sinking: the Guardian counts two sunken container ships, adding the Antigua and Barbuda flagged Cape Dao, while the BBC describes Cape Dao as a bulk carrier reported to be sinking after a failed rescue, citing maritime risk group Vanguard via Reuters, and notes the only other vessel reported sunk since February was the Malta flagged Safeen Prestige. The BBC put the wider toll at at least 24 seafarers killed across 106 confirmed incidents since the war began in February, citing the International Maritime Organization.

**Latest (11:50 ET):** The Houthis vowed on Wednesday to press on with attacks on Saudi airports and oil facilities, called those sites legitimate targets, and urged international shipping companies, civilians and workers to evacuate them. Three people with knowledge of the matter told Reuters that Tuesday night's strike on the Aramco base in Dhahran, which hosts the company's headquarters and a major operations centre, caused extensive damage, with a fire spreading across several floors; the Saudi energy ministry had described a limited fire with no injuries or deaths. Operations at Aramco were continuing normally, with employees working from home, according to two of the sources, and Aramco did not immediately reply to a request for comment. Riyadh's King Khalid international airport, hit four times in the past week including Saturday's attack that killed 13 people, has been closed until October 17, with airlines cancelling flights to Saudi Arabia. Inside Yemen, the World Food Programme said about 800,000 people have been cut off from life-saving food assistance as front lines shifted, including more than 600,000 people in Taiz, and the Red Cross said front-line hospitals in Marib, Taiz and along the Red Sea coast are overwhelmed, some taking more than 700 cases in a single week.

**Latest (07:51 ET):** The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted and destroyed a Houthi ballistic missile launched toward Riyadh on Wednesday, and Saudi civil defence issued two early warning alerts, for Al-Kharj governorate and the capital, before saying the danger had passed. The Houthis said they had targeted Aramco facilities in Dhahran, an Aramco refinery in Riyadh and King Khalid international airport. In the Strait of Hormuz, the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre has recorded 15 attacks between October 6 and 14, 11 of them in the past four days, and tracking firm Kpler put oil carried through the strait at just 2.9 million barrels on October 11, down from 20.8 million barrels on October 8 and a pre-war average of 17.1 million barrels a day. Iran's navy said it has expanded surveillance east of the strait, and a US military official confirmed to Reuters that Iranian forces have changed tactics, using unarmed spotters in small boats to call in targets.

Brent crude rose 89 cents, or 0.8 percent, to $106.58 a barrel by 0030 GMT on Wednesday, and US West Texas Intermediate gained 80 cents, or 0.9 percent, to $94.94, reversing Tuesday's late slide as traders weighed a fresh Houthi strike inside Saudi Arabia against the prospect of more Russian diesel. The site market tables, fetched at 03:45 ET, show the same direction at slightly lower levels: Brent at $106.38, up 0.65 percent on the day and 6.17 percent on the week, and WTI at $94.56, up 0.45 percent.

The trigger was geographic as much as numerical. Saudi Arabia's energy ministry said the Houthis attacked an administrative building belonging to Saudi Aramco in Dhahran, the city that hosts the company's headquarters, causing a limited fire. The group has fired ballistic missiles and drones at Saudi Arabia over the past week in retaliation for the kingdom's support of Yemeni government forces, and Riyadh's international airport has been hit four times, with the Saturday strike killing 13 people.

Analysts framed the session as a tug of war. Nissan Securities Investment strategist Hiroyuki Kikukawa told Reuters that supply concern from the Houthi-Saudi conflict is driving prices higher, while expectations that increased Russian diesel exports will ease market tightness are likely to limit sharp moves in either direction. He projected WTI would trade between $90 and $100 a barrel.

The background balance still argues against panic. OPEC said on Tuesday that Saudi Arabia raised production last month despite disrupted shipments, leading a sharp increase in OPEC+ output, and Russia's government said it saw no direct link between Ukraine peace talks and its diesel export deal with the United States. But with the Saudi-Houthi front now reaching Dhahran itself, the market is pricing infrastructure risk again rather than only shipping risk.
