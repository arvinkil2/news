A federal investigative committee in Nigeria has concluded that miners arrested during enforcement operations in Niger State died from suffocation in a severely overcrowded and poorly ventilated detention cell, and has recommended the summary dismissal and criminal prosecution of the Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps officers responsible. The 10 member committee, chaired by Jonathan Kure, presented its report to Minister of Interior Olubunmi Tunji Ojo in Abuja on Tuesday.

The committee found the deaths entirely avoidable, identified leadership failures and professional misconduct including by the state commandant, and accused officers of ignoring distress calls, maintaining unlawful detention conditions, failing to protect minors, and altering the detention area afterwards. It recommended prosecution, where the evidence supports it, for offences including murder, criminal negligence, torture and inhuman treatment, alongside investigations into allegations of extortion and mismanagement of exhibits, and a comprehensive overhaul of the corps' detention facilities, oversight and human rights training.

The compensation proposal totals N1.4 billion: N30 million for each bereaved family and N10 million for each survivor, with educational support for dependants, medical rehabilitation, psychological support and a formal government apology also recommended. Tunji Ojo promised to implement the recommendations and directed the immediate suspension of officers alleged to have cleaned the detention centre in an attempt to destroy evidence.

One discrepancy in the panel's own presentation needs stating plainly. The federal government had earlier confirmed 37 deaths, and the detainees included 14 minors. The compensation recommendation, as presented, covers 33 bereaved families, and reports of the presentation differ on whether it covers 41 or 44 survivors. The committee did not explain the gap between the 37 confirmed deaths and the 33 compensated families. Until the full written report is published, the compensation arithmetic should be treated as the panel's proposal as reported, not a settled count of victims.

The wider significance is enforcement legitimacy. Nigeria is trying to formalise artisanal mining and raise revenue from it, a programme that depends on enforcement against illegal operations being lawful and survivable for the people arrested. A panel finding that the state's own mining marshals killed detainees through detention conditions, then altered the scene, is the strongest possible counterweight to that programme, and implementation of the prosecutions is now the measure of the response.
