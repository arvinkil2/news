The Supreme Court of Western Australia has ordered separate meetings of European Lithium shareholders and optionholders for October 22, 2026, to vote on the company's acquisition by Nasdaq-listed Critical Metals Corp. The scheme booklets reached investors about a week ago, pushing the all-scrip transaction into its decisive phase.

The headline value is reported at $835 million, and the independent expert's assessment — included in the dispatched documents — concluded the offer is "not fair, but reasonable," a formulation that effectively tells shareholders the price is light but the deal is acceptable. Existing European Lithium holders would emerge with 41 percent of the combined entity. If the resolution passes and court approvals follow, the transaction is expected to close in November 2026.

The vote now comes with a fresh wound. European Lithium confirmed in its 2025/26 annual report, published Wednesday, that BMW has terminated its supply agreement for lithium from the long-stalled Koralm project in Carinthia, Austria, and that the advance payment BMW had made has already been returned. The Austrian press agency APA notes the company has held the Carinthian rights since 2011 without extracting a single tonne: neither the operating licence nor the extraction permit has been granted, and Austria's Administrative Court is weighing whether the project needs a full environmental impact assessment, with a ruling expected no earlier than 2027.

The strategic logic of the merger sits increasingly on Critical Metals' side of the ledger: the Tanbreez deposit in southern Greenland, one of the world's largest rare-earth deposits, arriving just as China's November 10 rare-earth truce expiry turns non-Chinese supply chains into a premium asset class. Through the delays and the collapsed BMW arrangement, the shares closed at 0.2455 euros, up 171 percent since January and about 20 percent below their 52-week high.
