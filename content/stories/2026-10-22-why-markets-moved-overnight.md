Dry bulk freight rose 4.09 percent to 12.99 dollars, the largest move in the tables. No story in this morning's reporting explains the rise: today's freight reporting is about tanker costs and dark fleet tracking, where the tanker freight index eased 0.15 percent, so no cause is claimed here.

Tesla rose 3.41 percent to 386.47 dollars and is up 5.26 percent on the week. The move came against weak reported numbers, not because of them: Tesla's third quarter profit fell 43 percent on flat revenue, and this morning's technology reporting reads the share price as the market pricing the autonomy and Cybercab option rather than the car business (see the updated Tesla story from October 21).

Brent crude rose 3.23 percent to 112.34 dollars a barrel, up 6.06 percent on the week, and WTI crude rose 2.71 percent to 99.78 dollars. The driver is in today's commodities reporting: a Houthi drone aimed at a processing plant at Saudi Arabia's Shaybah field was intercepted and its debris killed two people, the Saudi Energy Ministry said operations were unaffected, and US crude stocks fell 3.06 million barrels to 412.5 million (see today's story, "Oil jumps after Shaybah strike as inventories tighten").

The Hang Seng fell 3.03 percent to 22,935 points and the Nikkei 225 fell 2.65 percent to 62,908 points. This morning's reporting documents the same regional pattern in Seoul, where the Kospi closed down 3.5 percent in a chip selloff driven by AI valuation doubts, and notes those doubts also pulled Treasury yields lower, but no story in today's reporting names a specific driver for the Hong Kong or Tokyo moves themselves.

Nvidia rose 2.54 percent to 221.97 dollars, with the semiconductor index up 2.07 percent. Today's technology reporting gives the sector a company specific driver: Nvidia is paying to expand its supply chain, with six supplier deals, substrate capacity set to rise two and a half times in three years and a 1.1 billion dollar advance to KYEC for test capacity (see today's story on Nvidia's supply chain capacity advances).

Silver fell 2.46 percent to 56.01 dollars an ounce, and gold fell 1.2 percent to 4,100.20 dollars. Today's commodities reporting explains the direction: the market is treating the oil shock as an inflation and rates problem before a safe haven problem, so higher crude is lifting rate expectations and making non-yielding metal more expensive to hold rather than triggering flight to safety buying (see today's story, "Gold and silver fall as oil stokes inflation fears").

The cross-market read is a split tape, not a single risk move. Energy and freight rose on a physical attack, US technology rose on company specific capacity and autonomy stories, Asian equities fell on valuation doubts about that same AI trade, and precious metals fell because the shock is being priced through inflation and rates. US Treasury yields barely moved in the tables, the 10-year up 2.9 basis points to 5.15 percent and the dollar index down 0.17 percent, so the policy path is not yet repricing even as the commodity shock deepens.
