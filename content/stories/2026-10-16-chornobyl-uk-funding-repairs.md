Britain will pay for critical repairs to the Chornobyl nuclear power plant, where a Russian drone strike damaged the giant confinement arch built over the destroyed fourth reactor, the government announced. The New Safe Confinement, completed in 2016 with international funding of about 1.5 billion euros, is designed to contain the wreckage for a hundred years; the strike punched through the structure that makes the site safe to work around.

The repair funding is small money with an outsized principle inside it. Nuclear safety assets in Ukraine, Chornobyl's arch, the occupied Zaporizhzhia plant's cooling systems, sit outside the normal rules of war finance: they were built by an international coalition, they protect populations far beyond Ukraine, and their maintenance now depends on donors treating them as shared infrastructure rather than Ukrainian property.

London's announcement also continues a pattern in this week's file: the costs of the war's infrastructure damage are being distributed, quietly and bilaterally, long before any reparations framework exists to assign them.
