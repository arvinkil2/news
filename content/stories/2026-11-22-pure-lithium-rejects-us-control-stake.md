US Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick asked Pure Lithium, a privately held developer of lithium metal batteries, to give the federal government a 51 percent controlling stake in the company, according to documents reviewed by Reuters and a person with direct knowledge of the request. The company rejected the proposal.

At a July 29 meeting in Washington with Pure Lithium chief executive Emilie Bodoin, Lutnick said the government needed to control the company so its battery could be used by the US military and other agencies, Reuters reported. In exchange, $1 billion from Japan's investment pledge to the United States would be set aside to build a manufacturing plant owned by a special purpose vehicle controlled by Tokyo and Washington. Pure Lithium would operate the plant under contract and receive a share of revenue after debt service, but would receive no funding itself.

The company's counsel, Skadden Arps, wrote in an August 22 memorandum that "51% is far outside precedent and invites scrutiny," warning that the stake would likely draw public attention, risk future capital raising and outweigh any benefit to shareholders. Pure Lithium offered a 9.9 percent stake instead, which the Commerce Department turned down in August, according to the documents.

Pure Lithium's battery, which the company says is far more efficient than a conventional lithium ion cell, can be built without graphite, nickel or cobalt. The meeting was arranged by billionaire mining financier Robert Friedland, one of the company's largest shareholders, and had originally been called to discuss the company's patent dispute with Panasonic. The Commerce Department did not respond to Reuters' questions about the proposal.
