Chile's copper price slump is threatening President Jose Antonio Kast's efforts to rein in the fiscal deficit, as rising fuel subsidies and weaker than expected economic growth strain government finances. Copper, which accounts for about half of Chile's exports and a substantial share of government revenue, fell about 10 percent in New York last week, pushing prices more than 20 percent below their record closing high on September 9 before a sharp rebound on Monday.

Revenue from private mining companies rose 45 percent in real terms to 5.89 trillion pesos ($5.9 billion) during the first nine months of 2026, according to government figures, and transfers from state owned producer Codelco increased 30 percent to 1.74 trillion pesos. Together those sources accounted for 13 percent of government income during the period, helping narrow the budget deficit to 0.9 percent of GDP. Lower prices could erode that contribution while the administration absorbs additional costs from its agreement with truck drivers.

The government agreed this month to increase fuel subsidies following protests by truck drivers. The measures are expected to cost 279 billion pesos this year and another 523 billion pesos in 2027, according to legislative documents. Separate data showed gross domestic product contracted more than expected in the third quarter, and Kast, who took office on March 11, has provided few updates on his pledge to cut $6 billion in spending within 18 months. His administration has also abandoned a target to eliminate the structural budget deficit by 2030.

BBVA strategist William Snead told Bloomberg News that persistently high oil prices and declining copper prices are an unwelcome combination for Chile at a time when economic growth remains sluggish, with potential pressure on fiscal revenues, external accounts and domestic activity. The administration is also considering expanding private sector involvement in Codelco, a proposal that has drawn criticism from the Socialist Party, unions and opposition lawmakers.

Chile's deteriorating fiscal outlook could attract greater scrutiny from credit rating agencies as sovereign debt issuance is expected to reach $25 billion in 2027. S&P Global Ratings assigns Chile an A rating, six notches above Brazil, three above Mexico and four above Peru, while Moody's assigns an equivalent rating and Fitch places the country one notch lower.
