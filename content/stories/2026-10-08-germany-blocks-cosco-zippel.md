Germany's federal cabinet has blocked China COSCO Shipping's planned acquisition of an 80 percent stake in the Zippel Group, the Hamburg-based logistics firm that moves containers between North Sea ports and inland Germany, on national-security grounds. The decision, announced October 7, ends a review that has run since the start of the year.

Zippel, founded in 1876, operates about 200 trucks and employs roughly 350 people, organizing rail, inland waterway and road container transport between the ports of Hamburg and Bremerhaven and inland destinations. The company handled around 205,000 TEU in 2024 with roughly 75 million euros in annual revenue. Under the proposed structure, managing director Axel Plass would have retained the remaining 20 percent stake and continued running the business.

The economy ministry said selling Zippel to the world's fourth-largest shipping company would deepen Germany's and the EU's dependence on China and negatively affect supply-chain stability. Military logistics factored into the decision: in the event of a conflict, transport routes to Germany's eastern border would be critical for NATO operations and Bundeswehr logistics. The government had signaled "major security concerns" about the deal in late September, when Handelsblatt obtained an internal government memo, marked for official use only, warning that the acquisition would deepen strategic dependencies and could be used as political leverage. The review examined Zippel's software systems and sensitive data handling. Zippel chief executive Axel Plass said he understood the decision but stood by the business logic of the deal, and said daily operations continue as normal. COSCO did not immediately respond to a Reuters request for comment.

The block follows the pattern of the 2022-23 Tollerort compromise, where Berlin approved only a reduced 24.9 percent COSCO stake in Hamburg's port terminal under restrictions. An 80 percent controlling stake in a hinterland operator crossed a line the previous government had not drawn. The government is separately preparing stricter investment-screening legislation, and the cabinet approval signals a broader policy shift under Chancellor Friedrich Merz toward blocking strategic dependencies tied to China. It adds fresh friction to the EU-China trade talks running in Beijing this week.
