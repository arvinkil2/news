Latest: President Donald Trump told reporters at a White House briefing on Monday that the FBI is looking into Jensen Huang's affair with a Chinese government agent, the Associated Press reported. Trump said he had spoken with Huang and asked him about it directly: "I asked him about that and he said he had absolutely no idea that the person in question was a spy," the president said, adding that he believed him. "I know the FBI is looking into it in terms of something else, and I think that he'll be in good shape," Trump said, again calling Huang "a very honorable man." A syndicated account of the same briefing reported that Trump said he had called Huang "to see if he's feeling all right." The remarks change Trump's position of Sunday night, when he defended Huang while saying he had not spoken with him about the allegations.

In the market on Monday: Nvidia shares tumbled over 4 percent in early trading in the United States as US markets opened for the week, underperforming a broad rally in which the Nasdaq rose 2.7 percent, Al Jazeera reported with Reuters. Analysts quoted in the report were sceptical the controversy would force Huang out: Michael Monaghan, portfolio manager of the Founders 100 ETF, told Al Jazeera he saw removal as highly improbable on the current information.

Earlier on Monday: Nvidia shares tumbled more than 6 percent in Frankfurt trading, extending last week's selloff to fresh multi-month lows, Reuters reported, and at those levels the chipmaker stood to lose about 270 billion dollars from Friday's closing price, according to LSEG Datastream. The slide followed Sunday's announcement of a board investigation into whether chief executive Jensen Huang had been targeted by a foreign intelligence effort, and it put his future at the world's most valuable chipmaker under scrutiny a week before its earnings. In Seoul, Huang's daughter Madison Huang, an Nvidia executive, cancelled a planned visit for an Nvidia conference that was set to begin on Monday, according to companies she had been scheduled to meet, while Nvidia AI Day Seoul will proceed as planned, South Korean media reported.

China's foreign ministry answered the allegations directly on Monday. "The so-called Chinese spy narrative is purely a groundless accusation, slander, and a smear," spokesperson Guo Jiakun told reporters in Beijing, Reuters reported; that is Beijing's own position on the claims, which remain unproven. Nvidia's Asian suppliers moved the other way: TSMC shares jumped 6.5 percent on Monday, Samsung Electronics rose 2.9 percent and SK Hynix climbed 4.7 percent after last week's selloff in AI-related stocks, and South Korea's deputy science minister said the government expected no disruptions to contracted deliveries of Nvidia graphics processing units.

Earlier: China's state-run Global Times dismissed the spy allegations against Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang as a fabricated narrative, Bloomberg reported on Monday. The nationalist tabloid denied that Beijing had any link to Huang's extramarital affair, said American outlets, in particular The New York Times, were "going crazy" with speculative stories, and warned that the issue could hurt ties between China and the United States. In a passage Bloomberg quoted, the commentary said all it took were a few photographs showing Asian faces and Nvidia's previous efforts to push for relaxing trade curbs on China, and called that precisely how American media manufacture stories. The commentary is the first Chinese state-media response since Sunday's board investigation announcement; as a state-run outlet's account, it is Beijing's narrative of the affair, not an independent finding.

President Donald Trump defended Huang on Sunday night, telling reporters he had not heard that the Nvidia chief executive had done anything wrong and that he would take Huang with him on an Asia trip next week, Bloomberg reported. The defence came hours after the board announced its investigation, and it follows reporting that Trump was briefed earlier this year about Huang's relationship with the suspected spy and took no action.

Nvidia said on Sunday that it is opening an investigation into its chief executive, Jensen Huang, after he acknowledged to the company's board that he had been intimate with Alice Huang, the Megaspeed executive whom US intelligence determined to be a Chinese spy, The New York Times reported. In a message to the company, Huang apologised for his conduct, writing that he had been unfaithful to his wife and that there was no excuse for what he did. Axios reported the same two facts on Sunday: that Huang admitted a romantic relationship with a suspected Chinese spy in a message to his company, and that the board announced an independent investigation.

The board's statement, carried in full by The Verge, said the board is initiating an independent investigation in light of recent reporting that Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and that a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside independent counsel, will oversee it. The board said it has no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised, that its inquiry will examine all available information, and that Huang fully supports the review and has committed to cooperating with it. The board said it has confidence in Jensen and his leadership team.

Beijing's earlier response predates Sunday's announcement. On October 30 the BBC reported that China had rejected the original Times allegations as groundless speculation, with foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun calling the report an attempt to smear China. The Global Times commentary above is the first Chinese response since Sunday's announcement in the sources read.

Earlier on Sunday, The New York Times reported that Secretary of State Marco Rubio oversaw the drafting of the highly classified memorandum this spring that warned top Trump administration officials that Jensen Huang was in a close relationship with a Chinese spy, a role for Rubio that had not been reported before. The memorandum, based on electronic surveillance of Alice Huang, was circulated to officials with high level security clearances ahead of President Trump's trip to China in May, and Huang was initially left off the list of business and technology executives invited to join that trip, though the Times said it is unclear whether the exclusion was connected to the memorandum. A State Department spokesman and a White House spokeswoman declined to comment to the Times.

The scrutiny also widened beyond the Republican chairs over the weekend. Senator Chris Coons, a Delaware Democrat in line to chair the Senate Foreign Relations Committee when his party takes control of the chamber in January, posted on Saturday that Congress would investigate and get answers. Representative Seth Magaziner, a Rhode Island Democrat on the House Homeland Security Committee, called for Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify before Congress alongside Huang. On a separate matter, Senator John Cornyn, a Texas Republican, shared a New York Times report published Saturday that the Trump administration approved a Chinese firm, New Matrix, to assemble chips for Nvidia in Beijing, with the Times finding little evidence of meaningful operations at its listed address despite the Commerce Department clearing it on national security grounds. Cornyn called the report "outrageous" in his post. CNBC reported the statements on Sunday; neither Nvidia nor the Commerce Department responded to its request for comment.

Three Republican committee chairs in the US House of Representatives have asked Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang to appear for a joint closed door briefing on November 18. The letter, dated November 4, was signed by Brian Mast of Florida, who chairs the Foreign Affairs Committee, John Moolenaar of Michigan, who chairs the Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party, and Rick Crawford of Arkansas, who chairs the Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence. Huang has been asked to accept the date or propose an alternative by November 11.

The chairs want to question Huang on two subjects: Nvidia's security protocols against foreign adversary espionage, and his interactions with Alice Huang, a senior executive at Singapore based data center company Megaspeed International. The request follows an October 29 New York Times report that US intelligence agencies concluded Alice Huang was passing information to the Chinese government and had cultivated a close relationship with the Nvidia chief. The Times reporting does not assert that Jensen Huang knowingly took part in espionage, and neither Alice Huang nor Megaspeed has been charged with any crime. Megaspeed affiliates reportedly bought at least 4.8 billion dollars of Nvidia AI equipment between June 2024 and February 2026, a period when the company was already under investigation over suspected chip diversion to China.

Reporting on November 6 and 7 added a specific document to the case. The New York Times reported that a memorandum circulated earlier this year, based on US intelligence, claimed Alice Huang was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security to spy on Jensen Huang, and the Financial Times reported that the same memorandum claimed a romantic relationship between the two executives, who are not related. TechCrunch summarised both reports on November 7. The memorandum itself has not been published, and its claims have not been proven in public or tested in any proceeding.

Nvidia says Huang's interactions with the Megaspeed executive were ordinary business meetings of the kind a chief executive routinely holds with customers, and that he does not give customers national security information. An Nvidia spokesperson told the Times the interactions were "not unusual" and that the company does not share classified information with its customers. As of November 6 the company had not confirmed whether Huang would attend the briefing.

The timing concentrates the exposure. The requested briefing falls on November 18, one day after Nvidia's scheduled third quarter earnings on November 17, and the committees said Chinese infiltration and access to cutting edge American technology remain core investigative priorities for this Congress. For investors the issue is not the briefing itself but what follows it: tighter export controls, deeper end customer scrutiny, or restrictions affecting the data center customers through which Nvidia chips reach Asia.
