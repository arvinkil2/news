Scientists at the Max Planck Institute for Multidisciplinary Sciences in Gottingen have built an artificial cohesion system that holds chromosomes together in aging eggs, addressing the mechanism behind most age-related chromosome errors. The study was published in Science on November 26.

Eggs are formed before birth, and the cohesin protein complex that tethers sister chromatids must hold for decades without being replaced. As cohesin is lost, chromatids separate early, producing eggs with the wrong number of chromosomes. That error, aneuploidy, is the leading cause of age-related infertility, failed IVF cycles and miscarriage, and rises sharply around age 40.

The team's system, called TALhesin, uses engineered proteins that attach near the centromeres where cohesin normally sits and bind to each other, with a built-in cleavage site so the cell's separase enzyme can release the chromatids on schedule at fertilization. In eggs from 17-month-old mice, the share without any premature separation errors rose from 46.7 percent untreated to 74.1 percent with TALhesin, similar to levels in young mice, according to the study and Nature's news report.

The work is explicitly a proof of concept. The researchers have adapted the system to human chromosomes and shown the proteins accumulate in the right regions in donated human eggs, but they have not yet tested whether it reduces errors in human eggs, and safety for embryonic development is untested. Lead researcher Melina Schuh has co-founded a startup, Ovo Labs, which has licensed the technology from the Max Planck Institute.
