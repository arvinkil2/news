Hundreds of French high schools will begin a second week of closures on Monday as protests over dilapidated schools, overcrowding and teacher shortages build toward a nationwide day of demonstrations on Tuesday. Education Minister Édouard Geffray said 400 to 500 of France's 3,700 high schools would be fully or partially closed on Monday; organizers called for Tuesday's demonstrations after accusing ministers of failing to offer concrete measures.

The numbers describe an unrest that has outgrown its origins. What began in late September at a Paris-suburb high school over teacher shortages has spread to Montpellier, Nantes, Marseille and beyond: 24 schools have been burned or ransacked, 78 education-system employees injured — "something we have never seen before," Geffray said — and around 5,000 people, mostly teenagers, arrested since the demonstrations began, including 1,747 detained on Friday alone. BFMTV showed police-student skirmishes in Lille on Monday. The government blames far-left agitators for hijacking a student movement; Paris's police chief said in an open letter that those who launched the protests no longer control them.

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu responded with facility upgrades, clearer university admissions, and a fast-tracked "vandal-pays" bill making vandals pay for repairs. The backdrop is fiscal: France's 10-year yield briefly topped 5 percent last week — its highest since 2002 — as the protests compounded investor worries over the deficit-narrowing 2027 budget the government is now trying to pass. In a separate institutional milestone, Marine Le Pen's National Rally secured enough seats to form a parliamentary group in the French Senate for the first time.
