The European Central Bank is turning its crisis liquidity lines into standing infrastructure. Executive Board member Philip Lane told a Shanghai luncheon on Wednesday that the ECB this year decided to enhance the Eurosystem repo facility for central banks, EUREP, to make it more flexible and effective, introducing standing access in principle for all central banks unless excluded on grounds such as money laundering, terrorist financing or international sanctions. More than thirty central banks have expressed interest in the past six months, and the first foreign central banks completed onboarding at the beginning of October.

The terms are concrete. Onboarded central banks receive euro liquidity as loans against high quality euro denominated collateral, priced at the main refinancing operations rate plus a spread set by the Governing Council to preserve the facility's backstop character. A single transaction runs from one day to one week and may be extended, the maximum line is 50 billion euros per central bank, funds can be used without ex ante restrictions, and drawing will be possible from the fourth quarter of 2026.

Lane's market argument is the part rates readers should keep. Because EUREP offers same day liquidity of up to 50 billion euros in a single transaction, it encourages foreign central banks, which are price insensitive investors, to hold euro denominated debt, including longer duration bonds and bonds from all euro area countries rather than only the most liquid, and it extends to Eurosystem recognised supranational bonds such as European Commission bonds. Repo lines and swap lines are complements, he argued: swaps work through the bank credit channel against local currency collateral, while repo access is tied to securities holdings.

The timing is not incidental. The remarks were delivered on the day France's 10-year spread over Bunds approached 157 basis points. A facility that gives reserve managers a liquidity reason to hold less liquid euro sovereigns is, in Lane's framing, a transmission tool; in the market's framing, it is also a standing bid behind the bonds currently being repriced.
