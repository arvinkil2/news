US productivity growth narrowed sharply in 2025. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that total factor productivity increased in only 7 of the 19 major industries, with every gainer doing so because output outpaced the combined inputs of capital, labour, energy, materials and services. Wholesale trade led at 3.7 percent, followed by information and real estate and rental and leasing at 1.9 percent each, professional and technical services at 1.9 percent, and finance and insurance at 1.3 percent.

The declines were concentrated in goods and consumer facing sectors. Manufacturing total factor productivity fell 1.2 percent as output dropped 1.3 percent, retail trade fell 2.3 percent, utilities fell 1.7 percent despite output rising 3.1 percent, and other services fell 2.6 percent. Output prices rose faster than combined input prices in 12 of the 19 industries, a pattern the bureau notes is consistent with declining productivity.

Labour productivity told a softer version of the same story: it increased in 13 of 19 industries, but total factor productivity was the largest contributor in only four of them, wholesale trade, information, real estate and professional services. In the private nonfarm business sector as a whole, total factor productivity rose 0.8 percent in 2025 after 1.5 percent in 2024.

The rates relevance is unit costs. When output per unit of combined input stalls across most of the economy, wage and energy costs pass into prices more easily, which is the supply side backdrop to the December hike pricing covered in yesterday's edition.
