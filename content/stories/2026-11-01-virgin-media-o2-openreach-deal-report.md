Latest update, November 2: Sky News reporting carried by Datacenter Dynamics puts the likely scale at as many as 4,000 jobs from the 16,000 strong workforce, with numbers still being finalised ahead of third quarter results on November 3, when a cost reduction target is expected to be confirmed. An earlier Financial Times report put the owners' cost cutting ambition at 600 million pounds. Trade union officials at the CWU and Prospect have reportedly been briefed. The company declined to comment. For scale, Virgin cut about 2,000 jobs in 2023 and up to 300 earlier this year, while BT is working toward 55,000 job cuts by the end of the decade.

Earlier, November 1: thousands of Virgin Media O2 staff face redundancy as the company cuts costs, the BBC reported on Sunday. The number of roles has not been finalised, but the BBC understands the cuts will take a sizeable chunk of the 16,000 workforce, fall mostly on non-customer-facing roles, and come with a review of contractors and suppliers, with senior leadership changes already under way. The Prospect union, which represents staff at the company, called the cuts disappointing and very unsettling, and Virgin Media O2 declined to comment. The BBC was also told the Openreach deal described below is very close, and that the company is highly likely to announce in its results on Tuesday that it will stop all investment in fibre.

Virgin Media O2 is close to becoming a customer of its biggest rival. The Financial Times reported on October 30 that the Liberty Global and Telefonica owned operator is nearing an agreement to use BT's Openreach wholesale fibre network to serve new customers in areas its own network does not cover, and that it is pausing the upgrade of its cable network to fibre as part of a wider cost cutting programme driven by a debt pile of about 22 billion pounds. Neither company commented on the report.

The structure of the British market explains the pressure. Virgin Media and nexfibre together reach about 19 million premises, but only about 9 million of them have a full fibre connection; the rest sit on legacy hybrid fibre coax cable. Upgrading that estate was the company's central capital programme. Openreach's full fibre network, by contrast, already passes about 24 million premises and is on track for 25 million by the end of the year. Renting reach is far cheaper than building it, and the reported plan would have Virgin sell over Openreach only where it has no network of its own, leaving existing customers on Virgin lines.

The regulatory backdrop cuts both ways. Virgin had been set to receive a 1.1 billion pound cash injection tied to transferring 4.6 million homes to a network owned by nexfibre, but the Competition and Markets Authority signalled this month that it plans to block the associated 2 billion pound nexfibre deal for Netomnia, on which a final ruling is expected by December 15. ISPreview noted the tactical reading: surfacing an Openreach dependency underlines what follows if the regulator blocks Virgin's own build path. A month ago Virgin's chief executive was asking the same regulator to scrutinise BT's takeover of TalkTalk for entrenching BT's wholesale grip.

Markets priced the report before any signature. BT shares climbed 7.8 percent on October 30, investors rewarding wholesale revenue that costs BT nothing to build. No terms, prices or launch date have been reported, and until a contract is confirmed the story is a reported intention under financial pressure, not a done deal. But the direction is clear: in UK broadband, the binding constraint is now the balance sheet.
