OpenAI is treating the politics of its infrastructure as a campaign to be run, not a controversy to be weathered. The company is preparing a public push for after Tuesday's midterm elections, chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane told Axios, describing the midterms as a bit of a movie trailer for 2028, when he expects AI to be the issue in the presidential election.

The programme Lehane outlined mixes policy and presentation: developing AI speed limits, finding ways to help people experience AI as empowering in daily life, pushing the United States to lead on global standards, and rebranding data centers as AI compute campuses. He described the local debate over data centers as good and healthy, and noted that Amazon and Microsoft have said they will no longer use non disclosure agreements in negotiations with local governments. Communities should hold companies responsible and accountable so they really and truly get a transformative benefit from these projects, he said.

The backdrop is measurable resistance. This beat's November 1 roundup carried Data Center Watch figures putting almost 200 billion dollars of data center projects delayed, stalled or blocked in the first half of 2026, and TechCrunch reported this weekend that data centers are now a midterm issue even in Brooklyn. OpenAI's own funding round was cut from 30 billion dollars to 20 billion dollars last week, in the same news cycle as the semiconductor selloff on AI spending doubts.

A new name will not change a power bill or a water permit, and the rebrand is the weakest element of the package. The speed limits proposal and the acceptance of community benefit tests are the substantive parts: they concede that the buildout's licence to operate is now negotiated locally, project by project, which is precisely the constraint the sector's capital plans have been slowest to price.
