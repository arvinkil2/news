Thailand and Myanmar will hold talks on November 25 and 26 over toxic pollution in the rivers that flow from Myanmar's Shan State into northern Thailand, as Bangkok prepares tighter mineral import controls. The dates follow a November 12 meeting in Naypyidaw where Thai Foreign Minister Sihasak Phuangketkeow pressed Myanmar President Min Aung Hlaing for urgent action, and an agreement to accelerate a joint working group on water quality in boundary rivers.

The immediate trigger was the Sai River. On November 5 the river became so muddy that filters at the Mae Sai treatment plant in Chiang Rai province could no longer cope, disrupting drinking water supplies. Tests found arsenic in untreated water at 0.3 milligrams per litre, 30 times Thailand's surface water standard of 0.01 milligrams per litre. The Kok and Ruak rivers are also affected.

Thai officials suspect mining in Shan State is the source. Researchers have linked deteriorating water quality to mining wastewater, including from areas outside the Myanmar military's control, and satellite imagery and sediment modelling have identified two suspected mining zones. Myanmar's deputy permanent secretary for mining, Myo Myint Thein, said the government has issued no mining concessions or permits in the area under discussion and would not authorise operations whose wastewater or tailings could flow into rivers. That leaves the central problem unresolved: the suspected mines sit where Naypyidaw's authority is limited.

Thailand is adding a trade lever. Industry officials are drafting import controls that would add five mineral types to those requiring import permission: antimony, lead, copper, gold and rare earth elements, with the aim of tracing supplies to their source mines. Implementation is targeted for early 2027. The scale of the trade is not small: Thai imports of antimony ore from Myanmar rose from 76 tonnes in 2023 to 42,217 tonnes in 2024 and 68,919 tonnes in 2025.

On Sunday, November 15, Deputy Prime Minister Yodchanan Wongsawat visited Mae Sai and said Thailand has told China and Myanmar that mines operating in Myanmar need to build dykes to hold back contaminated waste before it washes across the border. Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul has already raised the contamination with Chinese Premier Li Qiang. The November 25 and 26 talks, and whether Thailand wins permission to install monitoring equipment upstream in Myanmar, are the next test of whether the agreement produces measurement, not just assurances.
