The Wisconsin Department of Health Services announced on Thursday that an infant in southwestern Wisconsin has died in the state's ongoing measles outbreak. The coroner listed measles as the immediate cause of death. It is the first measles death reported in Wisconsin since 1990. The department released no further details, including the child's county or circumstances, to protect the family's privacy.

As of November 3, Wisconsin had reported 431 measles cases in 13 counties in 2026, the highest number in more than 35 years. Nine people have been hospitalised. All but three of the cases were in people who were unvaccinated, according to the Associated Press reporting of the state figures. DHS Secretary Kirsten Johnson said it was with a heavy heart that the department reported the first death of an infant connected with a measles outbreak in Wisconsin in many years.

The infant's age matters to the epidemiology. The measles, mumps and rubella vaccine is not given in standard schedules before 12 months of age, so infants depend on the immunity of the community around them. An outbreak concentrated in undervaccinated communities removes that protection first for the youngest children.

Nationally, the counts diverge by source. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has confirmed four measles associated deaths in the United States this year, while state health departments have reported higher numbers. That gap, and the Wisconsin milestone, frame the policy question for the new Congress: whether the resurgence is treated as a vaccination coverage failure to be reversed, or as a series of local outbreaks to be managed one county at a time.
