UN climate talks ended in collapse in Antalya on Sunday as the European Union and other countries rejected a final deal put forward by the COP31 presidents, Turkey and Australia, saying it was too weak to advance the fight against global warming, Reuters reported. More than two weeks of negotiations spilled two days into overtime, making it the longest COP on record, and still no deal was reached. The last time UN climate talks failed to get a deal adopted by all was in Copenhagen in 2009.

Turkey's conference president Murat Kurum told the closing plenary he would not advance any new text. "There is nothing to do. We did all that we could," he said, and he later left the stage while country representatives were still delivering speeches. Chris Bowen, Australia's president of negotiations, called the outcome "very disappointing" and said disagreements over fossil fuels and climate finance had been the obstacles to a deal. UN Secretary General Antonio Guterres said he shared "the very profound frustration of all those who expected governments to rise to the moment the climate crisis demands."

The central objection was what the text left out. Ireland's environment minister Darragh O'Brien said the European Union could not support the proposed deal in part because it made no mention of the fossil fuels that scientists say drive global warming. "It is unconscionable that we would have a climate act that makes no reference to fossil fuel, no reference to transitioning away from fossil fuels," he said. More than 90 nations, including the Marshall Islands, the United Kingdom and the entire European Union, objected to the draft cover decision on that ground, according to Grist's account of the closing sessions. Saudi Arabia and other big oil-producing countries had lobbied to keep any mention of fossil fuels out of the text, observers at the summit said, and a Saudi delegate told the closing plenary he was surprised at the resistance to what he called a concrete solution to global warming.

The failed deal had acknowledged the planet's projected temperature overshoot and called for faster development of carbon-removal technologies to eventually reverse it, language oil-producing countries had been keen to include and critics worried would be used as a licence to keep pumping oil. Developing countries criticised the deal for not providing sufficient climate finance to help them adapt: it called for a tripling of such finance but did not specify from what baseline. It also carried a Turkish goal of expanding electricity's share of total final world energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035, from around 23 percent now.

The failure does not erase the obligations agreed at previous conferences, and Belgium's climate minister Jean-Luc Crucke called the outcome difficult but "not the end of multilateralism." This year's summit was the first since the UN's environmental body confirmed in October that the rise in global temperatures was set to overshoot 1.5 degrees Celsius. When the Paris agreement was struck in 2015, existing policies put the world on course for roughly 4 degrees of warming this century; a decade later the prognosis is 2.8 degrees, a trajectory analysts credit in part to the signals past COPs sent to business and investors, signals Antalya did not send.
