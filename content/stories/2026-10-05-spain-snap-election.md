Pedro Sanchez announced in a televised institutional address on Monday that Spain will hold an early general election on November 29, seeking a broader progressive majority after Congress rejected two emergency housing decrees on Friday, October 2. The call is announced; the formal Cortes dissolution decree is the next required procedural step. Sanchez said Spain needed to renew popular support to overcome vested interests and deliver social reforms, while acknowledging his government had made mistakes and fallen short of its promises.

The defeated decrees would have banned evictions of vulnerable tenants until 2030, extended a rent freeze on existing leases by two years, and tightened rules on short-term rentals. They were struck down by right-wing MPs, and the defeat followed weekend protests in 50 Spanish cities over housing costs, including clashes with police in Valencia. Public anger had been building since the September eviction of an 87-year-old woman from her Madrid home.

The prime minister faces a steep climb. POLITICO's Poll of Polls puts the center-right People's Party at 34 percent, the Socialists at 26 percent and the far-right Vox at 18 percent, pointing to a PP-led administration likely dependent on Vox support. The PP, which has demanded early elections since Sanchez renewed his hold on power at the end of 2023, reacted with a one-line post: "AT LAST." Sanchez framed the vote as a choice between "the far-right wave that drives regression wherever it governs" and Spain as "a bastion of openness, social justice, and environmental responsibility."

Sanchez used the address to defend his record, citing Spain's status as the EU's fastest-growing economy, leadership in job creation and green-energy investment, and his own prominence as one of the Western leaders most forcefully critical of Donald Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu. He also conceded mistakes and public "dissatisfaction": inflation has outpaced wage gains, last year's Iberian blackout dented the prestige of the green grid, the Ceuta migrant crisis raised doubts about Spain's international heft, and corruption cases continue to batter the Socialist Party.

The campaign scrambles Spain's fractured left. Deputy Prime Minister Yolanda Diaz is not running for reelection, and the Sumar junior coalition partners are rebranding as the "Broad Front" with a new leader to be unveiled Oct 17; Health Minister Monica Garcia is the frontrunner, with Culture Minister Ernest Urtasun leading in Catalonia. Whether Podemos or figures like former Barcelona mayor Ada Colau join the project is unresolved, and the Socialists need a united left to have any path back to government. For markets and EU policymakers, the central question is whether a PP-Vox government pivots Spain rightward on deficit rules, immigration enforcement, housing intervention and alignment with the Trump administration.
