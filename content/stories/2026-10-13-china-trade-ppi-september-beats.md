China's September price data, released by the National Bureau of Statistics on Wednesday, showed factory gate inflation accelerating sharply while consumer inflation barely moved. The producer price index rose 5.0 percent from a year earlier, up from 3.8 percent in August, above the 4.5 percent consensus forecast and the highest since June 2022. On the month, producer prices rose 1.2 percent, triple August's 0.4 percent increase. The consumer price index rose 0.9 percent year on year, up from 0.8 percent in August but below the 1.0 percent consensus, and rose 0.2 percent on the month, slower than August's 0.4 percent.

The bureau attributed the producer surge to higher international crude oil and nonferrous metal prices, with winter coal stockpiling and temporarily tight supply in major mining areas also lifting coal mining and processing prices. Inside the consumer basket, the same energy impulse was visible but narrow: gasoline prices rose 16.6 percent year on year, contributing about 0.47 percentage points to the headline increase, while food prices fell 1.7 percent, with pork down 10 percent and fresh vegetables down 8.1 percent, together subtracting about 0.33 percentage points. Core inflation, excluding food and energy, was unchanged at 1.0 percent, and service prices rose 0.8 percent, also unchanged from August.

Trade, reported in the same overnight round, remained the economy's engine. Exports rose 36.3 percent year on year in dollar terms to $447.18 billion, up from 25.0 percent growth in August and ahead of a 25.5 percent poll median, while imports rose 31.0 percent to $313.66 billion. The trade surplus widened to $133.53 billion from $119.09 billion in August, the largest since January 2025, taking the nine month surplus to $938.51 billion.

The policy read is uncomfortable rather than urgent. A 5.0 percent factory gate print driven by imported fuel and metals is not domestic overheating, and a 0.9 percent consumer print with falling food prices gives the People's Bank of China no reason to tighten. It does, however, squeeze manufacturer margins where weak household demand blocks pass through, which is why analysts expect the producer pressure to fade as the commodity shock passes rather than to become a consumer inflation problem.
