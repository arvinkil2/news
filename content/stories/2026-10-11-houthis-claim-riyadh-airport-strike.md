**Latest (Sunday, October 11, midday):** the strikes are now going both ways. The Houthis said Sunday that the airport under their control in Sanaa had been struck again and blamed Saudi Arabia, though they provided no details, per the Associated Press. Saudi authorities gave no immediate comment on the claim. The Houthi-run health ministry separately said a child was killed and six people wounded in a suspected Saudi airstrike on a house in Sanaa on Sunday; the ministry also said 91 Yemenis have been killed and 168 injured by the Saudi-led coalition in five weeks. The Houthi-run foreign ministry warned that further Saudi military action in Yemen would trigger a stronger response guided by reciprocity: "airport for airport, siege for siege, and escalation for escalation," per statements carried by Houthi-run Al Masirah. The Houthis also renewed their warning to airlines, experts, employees and travellers against using Saudi airports and airspace, exempting only Mecca and Medina. These are the Houthis' claims; none of the Sanaa-strike accounts has been confirmed by Saudi authorities, so treat them as the rebels' narrative.

## Latest (Sunday, October 11, mid-morning): flight chaos spreads to Jeddah and Dammam, projectile lands near Dammam airport

The aviation shutdown is widening beyond Riyadh. Saudi coalition spokesman Maj. Gen. Turki Al-Maliki said a projectile landed near King Fahd International Airport in Dammam on Saturday, hours after the Riyadh strike, per Gulf Business. Flightradar24 data at 5:34 a.m. Saudi time Sunday showed 271 departing flights cancelled at King Khalid International Airport, 73 percent of the day's departures, with the airport's disruption index at 5.0, the highest level; FlightAware data cited by Arab News put cancellations above 290 in 24 hours. Jeddah, Dammam and Madinah are absorbing diverted Riyadh-bound flights, with reported Sunday cancellations of 92 of 830 scheduled flights at Jeddah and 39 of 240 at Dammam.

The carrier pullback is broadening. Etihad and Qatar Airways cancelled several Riyadh services, Kuwait Airways confirmed cancellations, and Emirates suspended Dubai services to Riyadh, Jeddah and Dammam for October 11-12, joining Air India, IndiGo, Akasa Air, flydubai, the Lufthansa Group, Pakistan International Airlines and others that had already suspended Riyadh routes after the week's earlier attacks. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree reiterated on Sunday the group's warning against using Saudi airports and airspace, exempting only Mecca and Medina, and Saudi authorities moved schools nationwide to remote learning until October 15.

## Latest (Sunday, October 11, morning): claim of responsibility, Trump weighs joining strikes

Two Saudi officials told MS NOW the kingdom is requesting "urgent defense support" from the US "as soon as possible," per CNBC, as Trump weighs whether to join Saudi strikes against the Houthis. Any US involvement would mark the first direct American military action against the Houthis since Operation Rough Rider ended in May 2025, per the Critical Threats Project. Trump's exact words Saturday outside the White House, when asked if the US would back Saudi strikes: "We may. We're going to look at it." UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting told the BBC on Sunday that Britain is examining how to support Saudi Arabia but is not joining offensive operations "at this stage."

UN Yemen envoy Hans Grundberg strongly condemned the strike on King Khalid International Airport and called on the Houthis to end such attacks, while EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas called it a "horrible terrorist attack," saying attacks on civilian infrastructure have no justification. The African Union offered condolences to the victims' families and called for de-escalation, widening the international backlash as the Saudi-led coalition vows a firm response and Washington weighs joining retaliatory strikes.

Mohammed al-Bukhaiti, a senior Houthi political official and spokesman, confirmed to RT that the group carried out Saturday's missile strike on King Khalid International Airport, the first direct claim of responsibility after a week in which the airport was hit repeatedly. He vowed the Houthis would keep striking Saudi targets until Riyadh agrees to a comprehensive peace deal ending the war and the blockade of Houthi-held northern Yemen.

Saudi Arabia's General Authority of Civil Aviation puts the toll at 12 dead and 309 wounded, with several in critical condition, and describes the figures as preliminary. The dead include four Saudi citizens, two Bangladeshis, and one national each from the United States, Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Palestine and Sudan. The missile hit the complex housing the domestic terminals, and the airport suspended operations pending a full damage assessment. Flight-tracking data showed more than 300 flights cancelled on Saturday, and no Saudi threat-warning alert was issued before the strike.

The attack drew an unusually direct American response. Secretary of State Marco Rubio condemned the strike and noted that an American citizen was among the killed, while the US Embassy in Riyadh urged citizens to avoid the airport and surrounding areas. Asked outside the White House whether the United States would join Saudi strikes against the Houthis, President Trump said, "We may," adding that a decision would come quickly. The Saudi-led coalition called the strike a "fully-fledged war crime" and vowed a firm response.

Saturday's strike was the third on the same airport in a week. The two earlier attacks killed four people and damaged a Saudi commercial aircraft parked on the tarmac. The Houthis have declared Saudi airspace a zone of military operations, a warning that drew flight suspensions from carriers including Air India and the Lufthansa Group and prompted EgyptAir to suspend Cairo and Alexandria services to Riyadh.

The escalation follows a Saudi-backed Yemeni government offensive to retake Houthi-held territory along the Red Sea coast, backed by Saudi airstrikes, with the Houthis retaliating against airports and energy infrastructure inside the kingdom.
