Israel's Central Elections Committee certified the results of last week's parliamentary election on Wednesday, after a review of 9,069 polling stations found no irregularities that compromised the integrity of the election or changed its results, Al Jazeera reported with AP. Acting director Dean Livne said every complaint had been examined individually and that the results accurately reflected voters' choices. In the committee's five member presidium, representatives of Yesh Atid and Blue and White voted to ratify the results, Likud and Shas abstained, and the chair, Supreme Court Justice Noam Sohlberg, cast the deciding vote.

Speaking at the ceremony at President Isaac Herzog's residence, Sohlberg said those casting doubt on the results pose a real danger to Israel's democracy, and he rejected a Likud demand to discuss the results before certifying them. The certified results were submitted to Herzog on Wednesday, confirming the count set out below: the Netanyahu bloc on 53 seats, the bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot on 48, and the Arab-led parties Ra'am and the Joint List on a record 19, with neither leading bloc able to reach the 61 seats needed to govern. Herzog will hold two days of consultations with party leaders before choosing a candidate to form a coalition, meeting the smaller parties first, starting with Religious Zionism and then Shas and Yisrael Beytenu, with the remaining factions on Thursday ending with Eisenkot's Yashar and finally Likud, the Times of Israel reported.

Netanyahu's allies refused to back the certified result. National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir demanded a freeze on certification and on forming a government and repeated the claim that the elections were stolen, and Likud legislator Amit Halevi resigned from the committee alleging that some lawmakers had bought their seats. Those claims remain uncorroborated, and Qatar has rejected the allegation that it funded Arab-led parties as disinformation. If no candidate can form a governing coalition, Israel would hold another election early next year.

The head of Israel's Central Elections Committee, Noam Sohlberg, said in a video statement on Sunday that the results of last Tuesday's general election would stand even if claims of foreign interference were substantiated. Appearing alongside the head of the Shin Bet security service and the attorney general, he said the committee's position was that substantiated suspicions would not undermine the integrity of the electoral process or the election results as a whole.

The statement answered a direct challenge from the prime minister's own party. Likud has questioned the outcome, saying without providing evidence that Arab-led parties, which made significant gains in the election, stole the vote and accepted funding from Qatar. Qatar has rejected the allegations and called them a disinformation campaign. Opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot accused Netanyahu on Sunday of running an orchestrated and organised campaign to delegitimise the results. Police said two of three activists arrested before polls opened, alleged to have been carrying envelopes of cash to bribe voters and linked by police to the Ra'am party, were released on Sunday; the allegation is a police claim at this stage, not a finding.

The underlying count leaves no governing majority. Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset and the opposition bloc led by Eisenkot won 48. Israel's three Arab-led parties, including Ra'am with eight seats, won a record 19 between them, which puts them in a potential kingmaker position: together with the opposition bloc they would hold 67 seats.

The results are due to be officially certified this week. President Isaac Herzog must then nominate a candidate to form a government, and the dispute Sohlberg addressed on Sunday is therefore not academic: it is an argument about whether the counted result is the legitimate starting point for that nomination while Israel is also a party to the war diplomacy running through Doha.
