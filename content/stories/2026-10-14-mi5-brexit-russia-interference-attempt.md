The head of MI5 used his annual threat assessment on Tuesday to settle one argument and start another. Ken McCallum confirmed that Russia attempted to interfere in the 2016 Brexit referendum, pointing to pro Leave output from Russian state broadcasters Russia Today and Sputnik and to internet troll and bot farm activity that he described as a matter of public record. He also pointed to a confirmed Russian attempt to meddle in the 2019 general election.

He then drew the line that limits the political use of that confirmation. The referendum result, he said, is not in dispute, and there is a difference between interference attempted and outcome achieved. Downing Street echoed the point, saying it did not believe the Russian activity succeeded in 2016. McCallum framed the live problem as present day: Russian and other actors routinely try to sow or exploit division in British public debate, and the response should be calm and proportionate rather than alarm.

The remarks land in an active political fight. The prime minister had told the Commons on Monday that Russia interfered in the vote, a claim now being used in the argument over Britain's relationship with the European Union ahead of the second UK EU summit in Brussels on November 20. The security service's position gives both sides something and neither side everything: the interference was real, and it did not, on the available assessment, decide the result.

For markets the significance is indirect. Foreign interference findings feed into sanctions policy, media regulation and the politics of the EU reset, but Tuesday's statement changes no law and announces no new measure. Status: official assessment, stated publicly; no new policy attached.
