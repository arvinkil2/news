**Latest:** Iran's Revolutionary Guards said on Tuesday that mines laid by their navy exploded in the southern transit zone of the Strait of Hormuz, setting two oil tankers ablaze and halting their voyages, Arab News and the Seoul Economic Daily reported. A further Guards statement carried by Iranian state television said a third tanker, carrying more than 2 million barrels of crude and travelling with its radar and navigation systems switched off, had exploded and caught fire after striking a mine south of the strait. No vessel has been named and the claims have not been independently confirmed. The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations centre reported an encounter between a tanker and military personnel about 121 nautical miles east of Duqm, Oman, and US Central Command had already dismissed a similar Guards mine claim made on October 17 as false.

**Latest:** The attacks are doing more damage per hit. At least 23 incidents involving ships attacked in or near the Strait of Hormuz were logged in the first two weeks of October, with at least five reporting impacts to their engine rooms, against no engine damage incidents in September, according to naval groups in the region and security consultants cited by Bloomberg. At least two recently hit vessels sank last week, including a container ship struck in Hormuz. Shipping and maritime security officials said Iran has begun using more missiles rather than drones, and infrared guided missiles tend to steer toward hotter parts of a ship. Flows are still moving: preliminary Kpler data put the seven day average of Middle East oil exports fractionally above prewar levels, and Energy Secretary Chris Wright said on Sunday that shipments are at about 80 percent of normal levels. The constraint is tonnage, not crude: every disabled tanker stays out of service for months of repairs, in a market where few owners are willing to send ships in.

Oil prices advanced again on Tuesday, with Brent hovering around a five month high as traders priced a geopolitical risk premium from Houthi attacks on Saudi infrastructure and reports of vessel attacks in the Gulf. Brent futures climbed 55 cents, or 0.50 percent, to 110.10 dollars a barrel by 03:19 GMT, after rising as high as 110.70 dollars in the previous session to their highest since May 20. Monday's settlement in the market tables was higher still, at 111.33 dollars, up 1.62 percent on the day and 5.34 percent on the week, while WTI settled at 100.63 dollars, down 2.92 percent on the day as its November contract approached expiry, with the more active December contract at 98.93 dollars early Tuesday.

The physical backdrop has not improved. At least 18 vessels were reportedly attacked over two weeks, Kpler said in a note on Monday, and the analytics firm has put the daily average flow of oil out of the Persian Gulf at about 8.5 million barrels, down from more than 20 million barrels a day two weeks earlier. "Oil prices remain well-supported amid ongoing tensions in the Middle East despite relatively strong Persian Gulf flows," ING analysts Warren Patterson and Ewa Manthey wrote. "This suggests the market is pricing in a significant risk premium, given the situation's volatility. Clearly, there are no signs of this yet," they said of any erosion in that premium.

Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthis said on Monday they had launched attacks on Saudi energy, airport and military targets, including an Aramco site in Yanbu, the Shaybah oilfield, Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport and Abha airport. Saudi authorities did not immediately confirm the claims and Reuters could not independently verify them; the claims are carried here as the group's own account. A separate Houthi attack on a training camp in Yemen's Shabwa province killed 58 people, with more than 200 injured.

The policy offsets that softened prices a day earlier are losing traction. Friday's executive order waived key Russia related sanctions and tariffs, but President Trump said on Saturday he had yet to announce a final decision, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio said officials wanted more time to evaluate. "While the prospect of additional Russian diesel supply offers some theoretical relief, traders appear far more focused on the ongoing risk of further damage to energy infrastructure across the Gulf region," said Tim Waterer, chief analyst at KCM Trade. In the United States, crude stockpiles were expected to have fallen last week along with product inventories, a preliminary Reuters poll showed on Monday. The International Energy Agency said last week that Russia's crude production rose by 65,000 barrels a day in September to 8.43 million barrels a day, still about 870,000 barrels a day below a year earlier.
