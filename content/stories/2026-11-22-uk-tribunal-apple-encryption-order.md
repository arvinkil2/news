Britain's Investigatory Powers Tribunal has confirmed that the government issued an order to bypass encryption to access customer data held by Apple, rejecting the Home Office's argument that revealing the order's existence could damage national security. The tribunal said the amount of information already in the public domain meant confirming the order would do little, if any, damage.

The order takes the form of a technical capability notice, issued under strict non-disclosure rules that had legally barred Apple from acknowledging it even after details leaked. Rights group Liberty, which brought the legal challenge on behalf of two individuals, said it is the first known time the government has been compelled to confirm the existence of a secret technical order.

The history runs in two rounds. In January last year Britain issued an initial notice seeking access to encrypted cloud backups of US and British citizens. That demand was dropped after months of negotiations with the Trump administration, but in July Britain issued a second notice applying only to Apple's UK customers. Apple refused the original order and blocked UK users from switching on Advanced Data Protection, its end-to-end encryption feature for iCloud, rather than comply, and it has also challenged the second order. Apple told TechCrunch it welcomes the decision and repeated that it has never built a backdoor or master key to any of its products and never will.

Thursday's ruling decides only whether the notice can be publicly acknowledged. A trial on the substance of the case is not expected until next year, and a final hearing on what specific details of the order can be released is also anticipated next year. The government said the right to privacy was crucial but should not come at the expense of public safety.
