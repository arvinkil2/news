**Latest.** The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya group in northern Rakhine state, in violation of its obligations under the Genocide Convention, by nine votes to six. Reading the judgment over three hours, President Yuji Iwasawa said the evidence showed Myanmar's forces killed some 10,000 Rohingya, and the court found that its security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, perpetrated widespread acts of sexual violence and acts of extreme violence against children, and attacked Rohingya as they fled in large numbers to Bangladesh. The court held that the only reasonable inference from the evidence was that the forces acted with intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya minority, and that Myanmar also violated its obligation to prevent genocide, because it should have known there was a risk of genocide when armed civilians joined the military campaign. It is the first time the court has found a state responsible for committing genocide. Six judges, Vice-President Sebutinde, Judges Tomka, Abraham, Xue and Nolte and Judge ad hoc Kress, filed a joint dissenting opinion. More than 730,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh in the 2017 crackdown, and, according to the UN figures Al Jazeera cited, more than 1.3 million Rohingya remain displaced, including 1.2 million in the camps at Cox's Bazar. Neither report read in this pass carried a reparations or compliance order, and what follows the judgment, including any move at the UN Security Council, had not been announced as it was delivered.

Myanmar's position throughout was that the 2017 campaign was a legitimate military action against armed groups after attacks by the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army on police posts, that its forces acted in self-defence, and that the allegations were not credible and factually incorrect; it also argued that the Rohingya are not a distinct group under the Genocide Convention. The Gambia's justice minister, Dawda Jallow, said before the ruling that the crimes against the Rohingya, including mass killings, rape, torture and the burning of entire villages, were carried out with the intent to destroy the Rohingya people as a group. UN investigators said in 2018 that the Myanmar military had conducted its campaign with genocidal intent and named top commanders including Min Aung Hlaing, who took power in the 2021 coup. Amnesty International called the ruling a rare victory for survivors and the first international ruling finding Myanmar's authorities responsible.

Earlier. Fears grew in The Hague this week that the United States could sanction the court's judges if the judgment found Myanmar committed genocide, The Guardian reported on Wednesday. Judges took advice on protecting their assets from potential US sanctions, the paper said, because a genocide ruling could be read in Washington as lowering the legal threshold for the crime and so set a precedent for the court's separate case on Israel's actions in Gaza. The Foundation for Defense of Democracies, a think tank close to the White House, predicted a genocide ruling would lead the US to impose sanctions on the court. US ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz publicly warned the bench, singling out The Gambia's ad hoc judge Navi Pillay, this year's Nobel Peace Prize winner, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio attacked international law as an instrument of a global elite in a speech last Friday and gave the International Criminal Court until April to answer his criticisms before he imposes sanctions. No US decision to sanction ICJ judges has been announced; the United States has already sanctioned ICC judges and the ICC as an institution after that court issued an arrest warrant for Israel's prime minister.

The Gambia brought the case in 2019, accusing Myanmar of breaching the Genocide Convention in its treatment of the Rohingya after the army's 2017 clearance operations in Rakhine state. The court announced on November 3 that it would deliver the judgment at 10 a.m. on Thursday, November 19, at the Peace Palace in The Hague, in the case concerning Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (The Gambia v. Myanmar: 11 States intervening), with President Judge Iwasawa Yuji reading the judgment at a public sitting. The judgment is final, with no possibility of appeal.
