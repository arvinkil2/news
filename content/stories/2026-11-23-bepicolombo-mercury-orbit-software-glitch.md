After an eight-year journey and nine gravity-assist flybys, the European-Japanese spacecraft BepiColombo has begun orbiting Mercury, only the second mission ever to orbit the solar system's smallest and innermost planet. The European Space Agency confirmed the orbit insertion on Saturday, November 21, and said on Sunday that during the manoeuvre the thruster burn was shorter than planned following a software issue. The spacecraft is safe, and teams at the European Space Operations Centre in Darmstadt are working to understand what happened, with a further update promised on Monday.

BepiColombo is a composite of two orbiters that travelled stacked together: ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and the Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, Mio, built by Japan's JAXA. They are due to separate on December 9 and 10, with routine science operations slated to begin in April. The mission will study why Mercury, alone among rocky planets besides Earth, has a global magnetic field, and measure its large iron core, surface and polar ice deposits.

ESA has not yet said which orbit the shortened burn left the spacecraft in, or whether the separation and science dates move. The arrival burn was planned at about 37 minutes; the agency has not published the actual duration.
