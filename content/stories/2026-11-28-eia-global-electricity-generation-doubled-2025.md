The world generated 31.8 trillion kilowatthours of electricity in 2025, more than double the 15.1 trillion kilowatthours generated in 2000, according to the US Energy Information Administration's recently updated International Energy Statistics, published on November 27. The agency attributes the growth to population and economic growth and to rising global median household income.

China, the United States and India have been the three largest generators since 2013 and together produced 17.3 trillion kilowatthours in 2025, more than the rest of the world combined. China's generation grew at an average annual rate of 9 percent between 2000 and 2025, from 1.4 trillion to 10.7 trillion kilowatthours, more than a third of the world total on the EIA's preliminary estimates. The United States was second at 4.5 trillion kilowatthours, including small scale solar, and India third at 2.1 trillion, more than triple its 2000 output at an average growth rate of 5 percent a year.

The fuel mix behind the growth is the part that matters for commodity markets. Renewables were the fastest growing source over the period, rising from 2.9 trillion to 10.6 trillion kilowatthours, and on The Edge's read of the EIA data renewables overtook coal in 2025 as the largest single source at 34 percent of generation against coal's 33 percent. Coal nevertheless remains the backbone where the growth is: more than half of China's generation and 70 percent of India's still comes from coal, and US generation is 40 percent gas fired.

For the commodity complex, the release is a reminder that power demand growth is structural and concentrated. The incremental call on fuels and grid metals over the next decade sits overwhelmingly in Asia, which is why Chinese and Indian demand data, not OECD data, set the marginal price for coal, LNG, copper and uranium.
