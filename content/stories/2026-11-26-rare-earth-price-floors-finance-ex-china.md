Western buyers are paying steep premiums for rare earths as China restricts exports, and whether those premiums can finance new mines and refineries outside China depends on the price buyers will guarantee for years, Sprott analysts Paul Wong and Jacob White wrote in a November 24 report. High purity yttrium oxide, used in jet engine coatings, traded at about $1,650 a kilogram in North America on November 17, roughly 185 times the Chinese price of about $9. In Europe, dysprosium oxide fetched about $4,875 a kilogram, nearly 22 times the Chinese price, and terbium oxide traded at about $10,500 against $944 in China.

The constraint is not ore. China mined 60 percent of the world's magnet rare earths in 2024, refined 91 percent and made 94 percent of its sintered permanent magnets, according to the International Energy Agency, and few processors outside China can supply the separated oxides manufacturers need. A long term contract with a minimum price gives a lender a figure it can underwrite; a spot premium does not, because the gap is expected to narrow once new capacity opens.

The model contracts already exist. MP Materials has a 10 year agreement with the US Department of Defense setting a $110 per kilogram floor for neodymium praseodymium from its Mountain Pass mine, and booked $8.1 million in price protection income in the quarter to September 30. Lynas Rare Earths, the only major producer of separated rare earths outside China, had minimum prices in place for more than half its output by late October, including a floor agreed in March with JARE, the venture of Japan's Jogmec and Sojitz, and in May 2025 became the first producer of separated heavy rare earth oxides outside China.

Equity financing is moving the other way. The Sprott Rare Earths Ex-China ETF closed at $11.09 on Tuesday, down 30 percent from $15.76 on September 30, which forces developers raising construction money by selling shares to issue more of them for the same dollars. Wong and White attributed much of the sell off to investors cutting leveraged positions as markets grew more volatile, while net inflows into Sprott's fund still topped $100 million between April and November 17. China shipped an average of 19.3 tonnes a month of controlled rare earth compounds and metals to the US from November 2025 through September, down 75 percent from 76.8 tonnes a month between June 2024 and March 2025, though Beijing has suspended its broader controls through January 10, 2027 while the April 2025 licensing rules still apply to certain products.
