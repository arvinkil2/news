**Latest (Monday, October 12, afternoon):** Trump defended the diesel deal publicly on Monday, writing on social media that he had made the right choice for the American people, per Reuters. He framed the deal as a step to bring down high fuel prices, which Reuters reported are jeopardising Republican chances in the November 3 midterm elections, and he again blamed Ukraine's attacks on Russian refineries as a leading factor in those prices. The defence answered the bipartisan Senate statement below urging him to reverse course; Republican Senator Jerry Moran said Washington should be tightening sanctions on Moscow, not undercutting them. Zelenskyy answered the price charge on Fox News on Monday, saying diesel prices depend on the blocking of the Strait of Hormuz rather than on Ukraine's attacks, and he promised to hold elections if Russia agreed to a 60-day ceasefire, per Reuters.

**Latest (Monday, October 12):** oil markets took Trump's claim at face value overnight. Brent crude December futures fell 0.77 percent to $103.91 a barrel and WTI November futures dropped 1.26 percent to $90.69 on Monday, per CNBC, which was read in full. Neither Russia nor Ukraine has confirmed that any agreement exists: CNBC carries only Trump's assertion that "both parties have agreed," and France 24's live blog frames the announcement as Trump "unilaterally" declaring the truce. Zelenskyy, who had earlier told Axios the announcement was "news for me," said Ukraine supports the ceasefire and that it is important Russia support it as well, adding that Kyiv is seeking further details from Washington. Separately, The Hill and Arab News carried reports that Russia struck a Ukrainian power plant after Trump's announcement; those items were seen from feed headlines only and are uncorroborated, so treat the strike accounts as unverified until confirmed.

**Latest (Sunday, October 11, evening):** President Trump said Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an "energy ceasefire" effective immediately, writing on Truth Social: "Both parties have agreed. Do not break it!" He provided no further details, and the White House did not respond to requests for more information, per Bloomberg via gCaptain, which was opened and read in full. The announcement came shortly after Zelenskyy, in an interview on ABC's This Week, said Ukraine was ready to begin de-escalation "from any kind of steps," including an energy ceasefire if Russia agreed to similar steps. Responding to Trump's announcement, Zelenskyy said Ukraine supports the ceasefire and that it is important Russia support it as well, adding that Kyiv is awaiting details from the US; he had earlier told Axios the announcement took him by surprise. Trump had previously announced an energy ceasefire last month, though Zelenskyy later said no deal had been finalized. Russia has not confirmed the agreement. The diesel deal at the center of the dispute is meanwhile drawing formal opposition: seven US senators, four Republicans and three Democrats, released a statement saying Congress banned Russian energy imports in 2022 and that Trump's deal to import Russian diesel was illegal, adding the administration failed to consult Congress as required by statute. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney's office said the agreement runs "in direct opposition" to the peace efforts of Ukraine, Canada and their allies. Bloomberg noted the cargoes total about 13.5 million barrels of Russian diesel into global markets, the first US imports since March 2022 per Energy Information Administration data; Russia, which accounted for about 10 percent of global seaborne diesel before its July export ban (recently extended through October), had those volumes compounded by the disruption of flows through the Strait of Hormuz.

President Donald Trump said Saturday that Ukraine should choose a new president, delivering his sharpest public break yet with Volodymyr Zelenskyy and demanding an end to Ukrainian attacks on Russian oil infrastructure. Speaking to reporters at the White House on October 10, Trump said, "I suggest they get a new leader who could make a deal because he could have made many deals and for some reason he never does," adding, "I think it's time for Ukraine to get a new president."

Trump paired the leadership demand with an attack on Kyiv's refinery strikes, which he blamed for pushing global fuel prices higher. "He better damn well stop," he said of the strikes, claiming they had created "a world problem" affecting American farmers and ranchers. Ukraine regards the strikes as a legitimate campaign to degrade Moscow's capacity to finance the war, a position European officials continue to support.

The remarks landed one day after Trump announced a deal with Vladimir Putin to bring Russian diesel into American and global markets, with a Treasury general licence authorizing Russian diesel transactions through April 7, 2027. Trump said on Friday that Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel, followed by 500,000 tons in November and a million tons "immediately thereafter," with another 3 million tons to follow depending on refinery conditions. The announcement came after a phone call with Putin that was initially scheduled to discuss a suspected plague case in Russia.

Zelenskyy, through the Ukrainian Embassy in Washington, called the diesel arrangement "a weak decision, unfortunately, a weak decision by strong partners," and warned that easing sanctions on Russian fuel hands Moscow revenue for weapons, drones and recruitment. The criticism was sharpened by timing: Trump's announcement came as his envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner were meeting Ukrainian officials about a proposed war settlement, which Zelenskyy said made his team feel used "as a smokescreen."

On Sunday, Zelenskyy answered Trump's demand on the refinery strikes directly, writing on Telegram that Ukraine would be ready to stop strikes on Russian refineries if Russia stops striking Ukrainian energy infrastructure, insisting that any de-escalation steps be mutual and backed by genuine guarantees. The offer frames the refinery campaign as Kyiv's reciprocal leverage rather than an expendable concession, and comes as European officials continue to support the strikes as a legitimate campaign to degrade Moscow's war financing.

Trump also claimed Ukraine had lost 30 percent of its territory and that more than two million people had been killed, figures that were not independently substantiated. He maintained the war would never have started had he been president in 2022. The intervention is political, not institutional: Washington has no mechanism to select or remove Ukraine's elected president, and Kyiv has held no presidential election since the 2022 invasion. European officials said they would maintain sanctions and support for Ukraine, setting up an open divergence between Washington and European capitals on both the war's endgame and the sanctions architecture.
