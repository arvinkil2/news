Severe delays at South Africa's biggest container terminal are threatening the country's auto industry. Problems with a new terminal operating system at Durban Gateway Terminal have roughly doubled vessel waiting times since the operator, Manila-based International Container Terminal Services, took over in January, Transport Minister Barbara Creecy said last month, and delays are now approaching 20 days.

The cost landed on Friday on Metair Investments, a Johannesburg-listed parts manufacturer supplying Volkswagen, Ford, Toyota and Isuzu. Metair warned that the backlogs threatened its ability to meet contractual arrangements and that it may have to invoke force majeure provisions in its supply contracts. Its shares fell 34 percent in early trade on Friday, and Bloomberg reported that nearly half the company's market value was wiped out over the day.

Metair said it has used expensive airfreight since August, added road transport and overtime, and estimates the additional costs incurred and expected during 2026 at between 20 and 25 percent of the earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation it reported for 2025, costs it cannot pass on to customers. It said meaningful improvement in Durban's congestion might not occur before the end of the first quarter of 2027.

Durban Gateway Terminal, formerly Durban Container Terminal Pier 2, handles roughly half of South Africa's container traffic and is operated by ICTSI under a 25 year partnership with Transnet, which retains a 51 percent stake. Freight forwarders estimate supply chain delays of up to 20 days, and truckers report spending up to 22 hours in loading bays despite holding valid bookings.

The operator said it takes the concerns seriously and will meet members of the National Association of Automotive Component and Allied Manufacturers on Monday to discuss the disruptions. Its chief executive, Javier Lancha, said the new operating system platform has since stabilised. For an auto sector already under strain from cheap Asian imports and US tariffs, the meeting is the first test of whether the stabilisation claim reaches the quayside.
