Senator Flávio Bolsonaro won the first round of Brazil's presidential election on Sunday, taking 47.03 percent of the valid vote — 56.1 million ballots — to incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's 45.16 percent, or 53.9 million, according to the Superior Electoral Court. With neither candidate clearing 50 percent, they meet again in a runoff on October 25; the winner's term begins January 5, 2027.

The result was a genuine polling failure: most pre-election surveys had shown Lula ahead by a single-digit margin. Instead Bolsonaro swept the three largest states — São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais — and the three candidates who finished third, fourth and fifth were all right-wing, giving him a natural runoff voter pool. The congressional results compounded the rightward shift, particularly in the Senate, where Bolsonaro's wife Michelle was elected.

The reactions on Monday set the runoff's tone. Lula, 80, called it an "unexpected result" and said a new campaign would begin on Monday. Bolsonaro, 45, said the result showed Brazilians want change and told reporters he wanted to "free the Brazilian people." Robert Muggah of the Igarapé Institute warned the runoff polls — which show a dead heat — likely understate Bolsonaro as well.

The economic stakes are immediate. Public debt sits above 82 percent of GDP, framing the fiscal debate: Lula has promised a debt ceiling if reelected, while a Bolsonaro government arrives with a right-wing congressional majority that changes the budget arithmetic. The U.S. tariff architecture on Brazil — a 10 percent baseline with a 50 percent effective rate on many goods and a 10 percent steel deal — hangs on the winner, and Washington's posture toward the result is itself a market variable. Copom has eased the Selic to 13.75 percent into this election — the runoff result will reprice that path.
