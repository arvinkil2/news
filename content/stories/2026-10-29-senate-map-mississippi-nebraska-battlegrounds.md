The Senate map is widening in the final week. Senate Democrats are putting 500,000 dollars into Mississippi, The Hill reported, a state that was not on either party's planned battleground list, and Axios described attack ads flooding what it called Mississippi's shockingly competitive Senate race between Scott Colom and Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith.

Nebraska is the second surprise. Bloomberg reported that economic pain has turned the state into an unexpected Senate battleground, the kind of late breaking contest that appears when farm and household budgets, rather than campaign planning, move a race. Fox News, from the Republican side of the argument, reported fresh uncertainty for the GOP Senate majority as two key races tightened days before the midterms.

The new movement sits on top of yesterday's polling picture, in which South Carolina was tied, Minnesota leaned Democratic by double digits and Florida leaned Republican. A map that was supposed to be decided in a handful of known toss ups now has credible contests in states that held neither party's early spending.

Late money is a tell, but it is not a result. A half million dollars in Mississippi buys a closing argument, not a majority, and both parties have incentives to project momentum in the final days. What is verifiable is the behaviour: the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee does not spend in Mississippi in the last week of a cycle it expects to lose everywhere else, and Republican aligned coverage is conceding tightening rather than denying it.

Control of the Senate may still not be known for weeks after November 3, between slow counts, the Alaska litigation covered separately today, and the possibility of runoffs. The widening map makes that timeline more likely, not less.
