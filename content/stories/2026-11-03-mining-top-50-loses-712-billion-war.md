The world's 50 most valuable mining companies are worth $712 billion less than at their record $2.75 trillion valuation at the end of February, a fall of 26 percent that leaves the ranking at its smallest since November last year, according to Mining.com's Top 50 tally published on Monday. October alone took $226 billion, or 10 percent, off the ranking, on top of September's $264 billion loss, and the fifty are now worth $108 billion less than at the end of 2025.

Mining.com attributes the October leg to hotter US inflation data hardening bets on another Federal Reserve rate hike, oil prices climbing on the Middle East conflict, and a technology stock rout in the final week of the month that forced funds to sell metals to cover losses elsewhere. Gold had its worst October since 2008, ending the month at $3,884 an ounce, down 6.6 percent, and silver fell 18.2 percent to $49.39, below $50 for the first time since October 2025. The sixteen gold miners in the ranking gave up $55 billion between them; Newmont fell 8.2 percent despite beating third quarter profit estimates, Agnico Eagle fell 9.9 percent and Barrick 9.1 percent, leaving all three largest gold miners in the red for 2026.

Copper producers lost the most in dollar terms, $69 billion, a 13.3 percent fall, even though the copper price itself ended October at $6.47 a pound, only 1.4 percent lower on the month and still up 15 percent for the year. BHP missed quarterly copper estimates after a fatal accident and weather stoppages at Escondida, Antofagasta lost 16.1 percent after weaker 2027 guidance, and Freeport-McMoRan fell 10.8 percent to $89.7 billion despite beating profit estimates. Iron ore slid 6 percent to $91 a tonne, with Vale down 15.4 percent after third quarter core profit fell 20 percent and Rio Tinto down 12.6 percent. Uranium was the only metal to rise in October, up 3.5 percent to $92.55 a pound, and Cameco still fell 11.1 percent.

The correction has come on light selling rather than capitulation. Shares worth $243 billion changed hands in the fifty in October, the lowest monthly turnover since November last year and barely half January's $454 billion, which Mining.com reads as buyers standing aside rather than holders rushing for the exits. The price of admission to the ranking has fallen to $11.33 billion, the lowest cut off since August 2025.

Monday's session offered a partial counterpoint, and another divergence. In the site market tables, gold miners rose 2.67 percent, silver miners 1.50 percent and copper miners 1.22 percent, with BHP up 1.15 percent and Rio Tinto up 1.03 percent, even as gold futures fell 1.41 percent to $3,913.70 an ounce, silver futures fell 3.49 percent and copper fell 2.61 percent. Uranium miners fell 2.37 percent, the only mining complex in the red. The bounce does not repair the two month drawdown, but it does underline the ranking's central tension: producer equities are priced for considerably weaker metal prices than the ones on the screen.
