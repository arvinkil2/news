President Joseph Boakai used the opening of the Liberia International Mining and Energy Conference on Wednesday to announce three linked changes to how Liberia captures value from mining. Gold extracted in Liberia will be required to undergo primary and secondary refining at licensed domestic facilities, to internationally recognised purity standards, before export. The government aims to establish a gold processing facility within two years, and Boakai told mining companies that new entrants must comply first.

Second, a Liberia Sovereign Wealth Fund will be established as an independent investment vehicle, operating in line with the Santiago Principles, and funded by a fixed percentage of mineral revenues, converting finite mineral wealth into diversified domestic and global assets for future generations. Boakai did not specify the percentage or the establishment date, which are the two numbers that decide whether the fund is a savings institution or an announcement.

Third, a National Mining Company of Liberia will take equity and strategic ownership stakes in projects, moving the state beyond collecting royalties alone. Mines and Energy Minister Matenokay Tingban said the company is subject to legal and institutional approval, and that its commercial activities must remain separate from the ministry's regulatory role to avoid conflicts of interest. The administration also plans a $30 million airborne geophysics programme through the Millennium Challenge Corporation compact, intended to give the state its own geological data before it negotiates with companies that currently hold better data than the government.

The context Boakai gave is revenue: the government targets domestic revenue of $1.1 billion in 2026, up from $699 million in 2024. Liberia's iron ore sector is its largest source of foreign direct investment, and gold output is growing, so the refining mandate lands on real production rather than a prospect list.

Jurisdiction watch: this is the West African pattern in a new jurisdiction, the same direction as Ghana's local processing push and the mining code rewrites Appian Capital warned about this week, but announced as incentives and state participation rather than royalty hikes or licence revocations. The exposure question for existing producers is sequencing: a refining mandate with no domestic refinery yet built, and a two year target to build one, creates a compliance gap that the implementing rules, not the speech, will resolve.
