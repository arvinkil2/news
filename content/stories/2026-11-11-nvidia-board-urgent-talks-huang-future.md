**Latest.** The selloff has reached China's AI sector. Chinese artificial intelligence stocks fell on Friday as Nvidia's leadership crisis heightened fears that Washington will tighten restrictions on China's access to advanced computing, Investing.com reported. Hong Kong-listed Z.AI fell 10.88 percent and MiniMax fell 8.73 percent, Tencent fell 2.71 percent, Alibaba fell 2.35 percent and Kuaishou fell 3.91 percent, while the Hang Seng slipped 0.9 percent, the CSI 300 fell 0.1 percent and the Shanghai Composite edged up 0.2 percent. The concern is the remote access channel: United States rules restrict direct exports of the most advanced processors to China, but Chinese companies rent Nvidia-powered capacity through overseas data centres, and Reuters has reported Chinese developers and cloud providers moving to secure more of that capacity, including through Southeast Asian operators, before it can be restricted. The Huang controversy could build support for the Remote Access Security Act, which would give the Commerce Department authority over that channel. Domestic chipmakers moved the other way earlier in the week on the substitution trade: SMIC gained 2.4 percent in Shanghai and 1.7 percent in Hong Kong, Hua Hong rose 2.7 percent, NAURA advanced 3.1 percent, and Cambricon surged more than 11 percent with Moore Threads up around 7 percent in Thursday's session.

**Latest.** The selloff has become the longest in more than a decade. Nvidia shares fell 4.3 percent on Thursday, bringing the loss for the week to 11 percent as of Thursday's close, and are headed for a sixth straight weekly decline, the longest losing streak since 2012, CNBC reported. The shares have lost 29 percent of their value over that six-week stretch and roughly $1.6 trillion in market value since the slide began in early October, and Nvidia has fallen below Apple after spending most of the year as the world's most valuable company. D.A. Davidson analyst Gil Luria cut his price target to $200 from $300 while maintaining a buy rating, saying he expects a challenging transition over the next few months and that the market has appropriately adjusted for it. Oppenheimer, in a note on Thursday, said fundamentals would take a backseat near term even with solid earnings expected, and that Huang's departure could translate into a lingering share overhang. The next test comes on Tuesday, when Nvidia reports quarterly results and analysts project revenue up 91 percent year on year to $108.9 billion, according to LSEG figures cited by CNBC.

**Latest.** President Trump tried to keep Huang in the job. Trump called Huang on Tuesday, November 10, while Huang was still chief executive, and urged him to fight for his job, the New York Times reported on Thursday, citing two people briefed on the conversation, in a report carried by Reuters. Huang resigned on Thursday. The account is second-hand reporting of the Times story, carries no quotation from either man and no response from Trump, Huang or Nvidia, and it sits alongside Trump's later public backing of Apple executive chairman Tim Cook as a successor, reported earlier in this story.

**Latest.** The Senate Banking Committee has joined the congressional file. Chairman Tim Scott sent letters on Thursday to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and to Huang asking both to testify before his committee over recent developments at Nvidia, Reuters reported. Scott wrote on X that recent reports raise serious concerns about national security and the protection of sensitive United States technology, said he would consider subpoenaing Huang if he does not appear voluntarily, and said the American people deserve answers. A growing number of lawmakers have criticised Lutnick for the administration's refusal to add dozens of Chinese companies to a key trade blacklist and over the government's oversight of Nvidia's exports to China, Reuters said; spokespeople for Huang and Lutnick could not immediately be reached. House Foreign Affairs chair Brian Mast also told reporters on Thursday that congressional staff spoke with Nvidia on Wednesday night about a date and location for Huang's closed door briefing.

**Latest.** The Justice Department has not concluded that Alice Huang was a spy. Attorney General Todd Blanche said at a press conference on Thursday that the department has not yet reached a conclusion on whether Alice Huang was working for the Chinese government, while confirming that the FBI is investigating the reports, Reuters reported. President Trump, asked on the Straight Shooter podcast whether Huang should have resigned, said it had not yet been determined whether she was a spy, that if she was a spy the answer would be yes and if she was not it might be different, and that he spoke to Huang on Wednesday. Beijing has denied that Alice Huang is a spy, and she could not be reached for comment. Nvidia shares closed on Thursday at $166.19, down 4.34 percent on the day, after the morning's steeper fall, and were modestly higher in after hours trading.

**Latest.** The export approval fight has reached the Commerce Secretary's job. Reuters reported, as carried by Yonhap on Friday Korea time, that senior White House officials have discussed the need to dismiss Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who oversees export controls and led the push to approve Nvidia's H200 sales to China, hours after other United States media, citing a senior White House official, reported that President Trump maintains full confidence in him. Yonhap also reported that Lutnick recently argued at the White House with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who pressed him on why Megaspeed, the Singapore company where Alice Huang once worked as an executive, had not been placed on the Commerce Department's Entity List. The dismissal report rests on unnamed sources and had not been confirmed by the White House or the Commerce Department as of this update.

**Latest.** Congressional Democrats have opened their own investigation. In a letter dated November 12, Senator Ron Wyden, the ranking member of the Senate Finance Committee, and Representative Richard Neal, the ranking member of the House Ways and Means Committee, told Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick they are investigating what they call the administration's scheme to approve and tax exports of Nvidia's H200 chips to Chinese companies, and directed the Commerce Department to preserve all records on the Section 232 semiconductor investigation, on Nvidia's export licence applications, and on the department's communications about Nvidia and Huang. The letter says Trump allowed the H200 sales in December 2025 in exchange for a cut of the proceeds, collected as a 25 percent Section 232 tariff when the Taiwan-made chips entered the United States for security review, which the two lawmakers call an apparently unconstitutional export tax. It says the approval came after the president and Lutnick had reportedly been briefed that Alice Huang was a Chinese intelligence asset, that Commerce approved sales to Chinese cutout companies with no physical presence at their listed addresses, and that Commerce suspended a separate investigation into Megaspeed after outreach from Nvidia; it also asks whether Nvidia's direction of at least 26 million dollars toward the president's priorities, including the White House ballroom, influenced the approval. As minority ranking members, Wyden and Neal can request records but cannot compel them. The letter's account rests on the press reports it cites, and neither Commerce nor Nvidia had publicly responded to it as of this update.
**Latest.** Alice Huang was a part owner of a data centre that provided computing power to Huawei, Nvidia's biggest competitor in China, CNBC reported on Thursday. Huang, under her Chinese name Huang Le, became a co-owner as early as 2016 of Shanghai Hexi, a financial firm that invests in technology and data centres. Hexi bought 27 percent of the developer Shanghai Century Longteng in 2021 and held it until 2024; Century Longteng controlled 50.1 percent of Shenzhen Yifeng Technology, whose Shenzhen data centre's key customer was Huawei Cloud, and Hexi guaranteed the loans that financed the centre. Her husband Kang Wei, who founded Hexi, became a Century Longteng executive in 2022 and attended the signing of its Huawei agreement. The account rests on Chinese corporate records and media reports compiled by Tesari, a private intelligence firm that shared its research exclusively with CNBC; Tesari's Brian Andersen, a former Homeland Security Investigations supervisor, said the activity warrants a national security and counterproliferation investigation.
