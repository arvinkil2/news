The Strait of Hormuz changed hands twice in 72 hours, and the price of reopening it is now public. Iran's parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said Sunday that Tehran will not fully reopen the waterway until Washington meets seven conditions rooted in a June memorandum of understanding — including a lifting of economic sanctions and a complete stop to all hostilities. "The era of wasting time and dictating unilateral demands is over," he said, according to state media. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi added separately that a plan put forward by Tehran could see the route reopened within seven days if accepted.

The Foreign Ministry said U.S. proposals delivered through a mediator "largely reflected previous positions," centered on the nuclear file, while Iran wants Hormuz itself at the center of any deal. And the one near-term diplomatic track just slipped: Iran–Oman talks on a temporary Hormuz shipping lane, due Monday, were postponed, Bloomberg reported.

The closure was accompanied by continued attacks. UKMTO logged two projectile strikes on tankers on Friday and a third on Sunday — the LR2 tanker LIPSI, hit in the engine room — plus a Saturday-night strike north of Musandam that left a vessel burning with its crew evacuated; Iranian media report one crew member killed. The Joint Maritime Information Center kept the strait at its SEVERE threat rating, with deliberate attacks rated highly likely. U.S. transits average about 30 vessels a day against a 2025 baseline of 138, though Kpler's provisional data put crude flows over September 27–29 above pre-war levels.

Behind the rhetoric sits the incompatible-timelines problem: Trump told reporters "very good conversations" are happening — in the same week the Wall Street Journal reported he told aides to expect resumed bombing in November. The two timelines are incompatible, which is why the strait, not the battlefield, is where the war's price is being set.
