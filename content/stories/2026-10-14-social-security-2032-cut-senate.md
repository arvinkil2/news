The largest scheduled cut in American social policy requires no vote, and the candidates who would have to stop it are mostly not talking about it. Analyses from the Social Security Administration, the program's trustees and the Congressional Budget Office project that the Old Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund will be depleted by 2032. Without congressional action, benefits would then fall automatically by about 22 percent, because payroll taxes would cover only part of scheduled payments.

The electoral arithmetic is the story. The 33 senators elected this November will serve from January 2027 to January 2033, making this the first Senate class whose terms run through the depletion date. Bloomberg reported on Wednesday that Senate candidates are dodging the crisis even as it becomes a central issue of the election, and a Peterson Foundation summary notes that overwhelming majorities of voters say they want candidates to have a clear plan.

What exists in Congress is process, not a settlement. The bipartisan PROMISE Act, led by senators Dick Durbin and Bill Cassidy, would require the Social Security Advisory Board to submit a reform bill ensuring at least 50 years of solvency and would force Congress to debate and vote on it under a fast track, with the same trigger repeating each decade. On current average benefits, reporting on the bill puts the avoided cut at roughly 450 dollars a month per recipient from 2032. A separate House bill would create a 13 member commission whose 75 year plan would need nine votes and then an up or down congressional vote. Neither has passed.

Other proposals in circulation include lifting or eliminating the payroll tax cap, currently 12.4 percent on earnings up to 184,500 dollars, an idea backed in different forms by senators from both parties. Until a candidate or a chamber commits to a specific mix of revenue and benefits, the default policy remains the automatic cut. Status: projections official, legislation proposed and unpassed, campaign engagement reported as minimal.
