The Bank of Russia kept its key rate at 14 percent at its October 23 board meeting, a second consecutive hold after ten straight cuts had brought the rate down from a mid-2025 high of 21 percent, and the decision was unanimous in market expectations: all 20 analysts polled by Reuters had forecast it. The substance was in the forecasts. The bank raised its 2026 inflation forecast to 7.1 to 7.6 percent, from 6 to 7 percent previously, and raised the average key rate it expects for 2027 to 12.5 to 13.5 percent, a path that implies only modest easing next year. It still expects inflation to return to its 4 percent target in 2027.

The bank was explicit about what went wrong. "Underlying price growth has not yet decreased" and remains in a range of 5 to 6 percent annualised, it said, "primarily due to the impact of a temporary reduction in production capacities in certain sectors," its reference to Ukrainian attacks on oil refineries and logistics hubs. Petrol prices have risen 21 percent since the start of 2026 and diesel 13.4 percent, Reuters reported, and the bank named fuel prices as a driver of overall price dynamics and a longer contraction of production capacity as the main pro-inflationary risk. Annual inflation stood at 6.4 percent in September, Dow Jones reported, with consumer prices up 6.2 percent since the start of the year.

The rest of the forecast moved the same way. The bank narrowed its 2026 GDP growth forecast to 0.5 to 1.0 percent and raised its 2026 Russian oil price assumption to 72 dollars a barrel from 60 dollars, Interfax reported. Markets read the package as hawkish: a T-Investments analyst quoted by Reuters pointed to declines in both the stock index and the government bond market after the release, calling the outlook revision harsh. The ruble was little changed against the yuan on the decision itself. For borrowers, the message is that the easing cycle is paused at a restrictive level for longer than the path set out earlier this year, with the next move dependent on whether the fuel-driven price pressure fades as the bank assumes.
