**Latest (Sunday evening):** The silence regime has now formally ended. Dmytro Lubinets, Ukraine's parliamentary commissioner for human rights, said the regime, in force from 03:00 on October 27 to 22:59 on November 1, was complete, that the parties kept the commitments they had undertaken, and that this made it possible to deliver humanitarian aid to Oleshky and to bring out civilian residents who wanted to leave safely. The oldest person evacuated was 94 and the youngest just over one year old, and residents' pets were taken out with them. Lubinets said he and Russia's human rights commissioner Yana Lantratova, in work coordinated by US First Lady Melania Trump, are now working out how to continue similar humanitarian steps, and that preserving this mechanism of interaction matters so they can go on helping people and saving lives. No new operation or date was announced, and neither Ukrainian report carried a Russian official reaction to the completion. UNIAN, reporting the Ukrainian military's account, said Russian forces used the pause to rotate units, strengthen positions, install anti-drone protection over roads, reinforce logistics and bring in ammunition; that is Kyiv's military's claim and is not independently confirmed.

**Latest (Sunday):** The six-day ceasefire was due to end late on Sunday, with the evacuation complete and hundreds of residents reunited with loved ones over the weekend in northern Ukraine. Both sides called the evacuation mission a success. Pope Leo XIV said on Sunday he hoped the cooperation that implemented the ceasefire, described in the reporting as the most significant since 2022, would support broader peace efforts and strengthen willingness to engage in dialogue. Ukrainian observers said the Russian military used the truce to reinforce its positions and rotate the troops garrisoned in Oleshky, and that they expect a renewed Russian attack; that assessment is Kyiv-side and unconfirmed.

**Latest (Saturday evening):** Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said the completed Oleshky mission could serve as a confidence-building measure and a precondition for further steps in the peace process. "If fighting can be stopped in one place, it can be stopped everywhere and forever. Ukraine is ready. We expect the same from Russia," he wrote, adding that the evacuees had crossed the state border in the Chernihiv region, that those who chose to stay in Oleshky must have unimpeded humanitarian access, and thanking the United States, First Lady Melania Trump and Pope Leo XIV for their roles in the mission. The local ceasefire itself is due to expire on Sunday evening, November 1.

Hundreds of residents of Oleshky, a town on the Russian held bank of the Dnipro opposite Kherson, left on Friday during a local ceasefire and flew onward to Belarus, from where they were to cross into Ukrainian held territory. David Arakhamia, a senior Ukrainian negotiator, said the fourth and last plane had landed in Gomel on Saturday and that within hours all the evacuated citizens, travelling with a Ukrainian delegation, would be on Ukrainian territory. The Belarusian state agency Belta reported three planes; the counts differ and neither side has published a passenger manifest.

The ceasefire, which Ukrainian and Russian officials both say has held without violations since Tuesday, is described in the reporting as the first of its kind and the longest humanitarian pause of the war. It was reached through mediation efforts by US First Lady Melania Trump, with the involvement of the Pope. The pause allowed roads into Oleshky to be demined and food, medicines and other supplies to be brought in. Supply routes had been disrupted by fighting and drones, food deliveries had stopped in May according to residents, and hundreds of people were starving. Ukrainian officials say about 1,700 people remained in the town, from a pre-war population of about 24,000; other local estimates put the remaining population nearer 2,000, and hundreds chose to stay.

The United Nations, in its account of October 30, called the ceasefire a source of hope for civilians while noting that attacks on aid vehicles continue elsewhere. The Kremlin has characterised the Oleshky arrangement as a special case rather than a signal of a wider ceasefire, and Sybiha's statement is Kyiv's argument, made on the day the mission closed, that the same mechanism can be extended to the whole front.

What the episode establishes is narrower than a peace process and more concrete: a named mediator, a defined perimeter and a shared interest in one town's civilians produced a pause both armies kept for days, in a war where broader truces have repeatedly failed. Kyiv has now formally proposed scaling it; whether Moscow answers, and whether the local truce is extended past its Sunday expiry, are the developments to watch.
