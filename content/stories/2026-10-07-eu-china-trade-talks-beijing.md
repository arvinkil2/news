**Latest (Saturday, October 10):** Sefcovic's post-talks remarks added the numbers the joint statement left out. He said the hybrid-vehicle understanding aims to cut Chinese hybrid and plug-in hybrid exports to the EU by more than half over four years, and that this was the first time Beijing had accepted moderating its exports without first being forced through trade tension, though the mechanism remains undisclosed and needs political endorsement from EU leaders meeting in Brussels next Thursday. On rare earths, he described the commitment as making the existing fast-track licensing system smoother and more user-friendly for European companies, with greater predictability and transparency. On market access, both sides will explore most-favoured-nation tariff cuts on goods from car parts to olive oil and footwear, covering almost 4 billion euros in current export value and an estimated 225 million euros or more in duty savings, figures two separate reports attributed to the Commission's account of the talks. Europe's carmakers welcomed the package: ACEA said a sharp, sudden surge of Chinese hybrids would be highly disruptive to the European economy and called the announcement a help toward an orderly transition. Sefcovic's own verdict set the terms for next week: this is far from the end, a crucial first step but only a first step in rebalancing. The full 16-point annex also records an intellectual-property working group, advance warning on export-control listing and delisting procedures, a technical dialogue on the EU's Foreign Subsidies Regulation, and China's pledge to accelerate recognition of the regionalisation principle for eligible EU member states affected by bluetongue.

**Latest (Friday, October 9, midday):** The European Commission and China's Commerce Ministry published a joint statement closing the second Trade and Investment Consultations, co-chaired by Maros Sefcovic and Wang Wentao in Beijing on October 8-9, with a list of deliverables and understandings. The headline items: an understanding on trade in hybrid vehicles "in a WTO-compliant manner," which lands the dispute that opened with Beijing refusing EU requests for voluntary hybrid-export restraints; a Chinese pledge to continue facilitating rare-earth and permanent-magnet export licences to the EU via a fast-track mechanism, alongside an EU pledge to help Member States facilitate priority dual-use licences to China; and a third ministerial meeting agreed for March 2027, with a ministerial video-conference in January. On the BEV anti-subsidy track, the statement recalled the guidance document on price undertakings and said procedures on corporate price undertakings and reviews would continue, while China agreed to accelerate recognition of the regionalisation principle for eligible EU Member States with a view to lifting bluetongue-related restrictions on animal products. The EU also welcomed China's effort to increase medical-device imports from the EU, including a match-making event at the 9th CIIE, and clarified that its guidance on EU funds for clean-energy projects involving certain inverters is country-neutral.

Read carefully, this is a process-heavy package, not a breakthrough. The rare-earth language commits China to keep facilitating licences through the fast track, but says nothing explicit about the moratorium expiry that Brussels Signal reported as November 10, and the EV file remains in the price-undertaking procedure rather than a concluded deal. That is enough to let Brussels claim the October deadline produced results ahead of the October 15-16 European Council, but the defensive instruments and the Franco-German retaliation proposal stay live alternatives if the deliverables stall. Sefcovic's separate press remarks did not render on the Commission's Press Corner; the details of his briefing (see the October 10 update above) come via press reporting.

**Latest (Friday, October 9, morning):** The council's second and final day is under way in Beijing, and as of this morning neither the European Commission nor China's Commerce Ministry had published a joint statement, readout or commitment. That silence matters: Brussels set October as the deadline for "tangible results" when the council launched in June, and Commission spokespeople have said the outcome must be credible. If the talks close with no public deliverable, EU leaders will take stock of that absence at the European Council on October 15-16, where the defensive instruments Sefcovic has threatened and the Franco-German rapid-retaliation proposal are the waiting alternatives. The rare-earth export-control moratorium, which Brussels Signal reported expires November 10, is the most time-sensitive ask, and nothing announced so far indicates its extension.

**Latest (Thursday, October 8, 10:44pm ET):** China's central bank weighed into the talks directly, issuing a statement in Chinese and English rebutting the EU-centered claim that the yuan is undervalued. The People's Bank of China said it has neither the need nor the intent to gain competitive advantage through depreciation, argued that China's export competitiveness comes from industrial competitiveness rather than exchange-rate policy, dismissed IMF real-effective-exchange-rate estimates as misused as official evidence, and pledged to start reporting foreign-exchange-market operations data to the IMF from next year. The statement landed as Sefcovic began two days of talks with Wang Wentao, against a backdrop of EU officials increasingly blaming the exchange rate for the trade imbalance. The yuan has strengthened against the dollar and euro this year while the euro has fallen more than 10 percent against the yuan from its January high, though IMF estimates put the yuan's REER 12 to 20 percent below competitors. ING's Francesco Pesole said he expects no major policy shift and read the statement as Beijing signaling it may act more forcefully to curb further yuan appreciation.

**Latest (Thursday, October 8):** As the council's first day wrapped up, the Financial Times reported that Brussels is now hoping to get Beijing to accept a unilateral EU measure capping hybrid-car imports, after China rejected the EU's request for voluntary export restraints. The ask marks the latest evolution in the dispute: the EU asked Beijing to cap hybrid exports voluntarily, Beijing refused, and Brussels is now effectively seeking Beijing's acquiescence to a cap imposed from the EU side. No joint statement with commitments has emerged yet; the session runs through October 9.

The second session of the EU-China Trade and Investment Council opens in Beijing today, with European Trade Commissioner Maros Sefcovic co-chairing alongside Chinese Commerce Minister Wang Wentao. Brussels set October as the deadline for tangible results when the council was launched in June, and Commission officials have said the outcome must be credible. The EU's trade deficit in goods with China was about 360 billion euros in 2025, roughly a billion euros a day, and it widened further in the first half of 2026.

The EU's asks are long-standing: curbs on subsidised Chinese imports in sectors such as automobiles, steel, batteries, chemicals and medical devices; fairer access for European firms in China, including public contracts; and reliable supplies of rare earths. The moratorium on Chinese rare-earth export controls expires in the coming weeks, and securing its extension is the most time-sensitive item on the table. Beijing has already refused the EU's request for voluntary caps on hybrid-vehicle exports.

The council convenes amid open escalation on both sides. Days before the visit, Beijing opened an anti-dumping probe into EU p-nitrotoluene. On October 5, French President Emmanuel Macron and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz wrote jointly to Commission President Ursula von der Leyen proposing new EU powers to retaliate quickly against economic coercion, including measures that could cut off market access within 24 hours. The letter does not name China, but Beijing is the evident target, and the Commission called it a valuable contribution. For Germany, long cautious because its carmakers depend on the Chinese market, the joint letter marks a clear shift.

China's Foreign Ministry responded on October 7 that bilateral industrial and supply chains are highly integrated and mutually dependent, urging that concerns be resolved through dialogue. Beijing also warned it would respond firmly if the EU restricted Chinese businesses. Sefcovic has said Brussels could resort to defensive trade instruments if negotiations fail. Separately, Germany's economy ministry vetoed COSCO's planned 80-percent stake in Hamburg logistics firm Zippel on October 7, citing supply-chain dependence and NATO logistics security.

What the council produces today and tomorrow decides the next move. EU leaders will discuss the outcome at the European Council on October 15-16. A joint statement with commitments on the rare-earth moratorium, import management or market access would count as the tangible results Brussels demanded. No statement, or a statement without commitments, opens the way for the defensive instruments Sefcovic has threatened and for the France-Germany retaliation tool to advance.
