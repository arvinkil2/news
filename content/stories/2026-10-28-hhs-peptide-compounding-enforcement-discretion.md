The Department of Health and Human Services has announced an interim enforcement discretion policy under which it does not intend to take action against a state-licensed pharmacy, a federal facility or a licensed physician compounding a drug product using a peptide bulk drug substance listed in the policy's appendix, while the list of bulk substances permitted under section 503A of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act is developed through rulemaking. The notice, signed by Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and filed on October 28 for publication in the Federal Register on October 30, says HHS acted because of significant interest in these peptides and the lack of approved products, and warns that it may revisit the policy if a safety signal or product quality issue arises. STAT reported that the policy covers six peptides that an FDA advisory committee voted in July to recommend for compounding, and comments on the draft policy can be filed on regulations.gov under docket HHS-OASH-2026-0496 until two weeks after publication.

Update, October 30: the six peptides are now named in reporting of the policy. BioPharma Dive reported on October 29 that the July advisory panel narrowly voted to lift restrictions on KPV and TB-500, promoted for wound healing, BPC-157 for ulcerative colitis, MOTS-c for weight loss, Semax for migraines and Epitalon for insomnia, and voted against a seventh, emideltide, which is not included. The same reporting explains the mechanism: FDA officials would not support a regulation calling the peptides safe, so the Secretary used enforcement discretion instead of rulemaking, after the agency's own reviewers had advised against broader use in July briefing documents. Public Citizen's health research group director Robert Steinbrook called the move reckless and unilateral, and Americans for Safe and Effective Medicines argued a legal pathway does not resolve untested products or safety risks. Leerink Partners analyst Michael Cherny called the notice a yellow light rather than a green light for telehealth companies such as Hims and Hers, having previously estimated the peptide telehealth market at $1.1 billion to $2.2 billion a year. HHS is taking comments on the policy for two weeks.
