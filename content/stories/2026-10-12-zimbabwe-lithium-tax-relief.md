Zimbabwe's lithium miners are back at the government's door on taxes. The Lithium Producers' Association, chaired by Innocent Rukweza, has warned that a cumulative tax burden is threatening the survival of operations and future investment, with producers arguing that nearly 40 percent of their sales revenue is absorbed by taxes and levies before a dollar is spent on labor, power or equipment. The tax stack includes a 10 percent export tax on unbeneficiated lithium, 7 percent royalties, a 3 percent community development levy, 1 percent marketing fees to the Minerals Marketing Corporation of Zimbabwe and 15.5 percent VAT on applicable transactions, plus corporate and payroll taxes and foreign-currency retention requirements. The industry's complaint is sharpened by the royalty calculation: producers say Harare prices royalties off the more valuable lithium carbonate rather than the concentrate Zimbabwe actually produces.

The fiscal fight is running into the beneficiation timetable. Producers face a January 1, 2027 deadline to shift from concentrate exports to processed lithium products, after a February suspension of raw concentrate exports and a 10 percent export tax on shipments. In June, the industry asked for a six-month delay to June 2027; Mines Minister Polite Kambamura rejected a postponement in July. Only one facility, Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt's US$400 million Arcadia lithium sulphate plant at Prospect's Arcadia mine, is complete and exporting chemicals (since April 2026, at about 50,000 tonnes a year capacity); Sinomine's Bikita Minerals and Sichuan Yahua's Kamativi are still building, and state-owned Sandawana is at feasibility stage.

The stakes are visible in the export data. Zimbabwe's non-gold mineral export earnings have roughly doubled to US$4.74 billion, with lithium overtaking PGMs, and the MMCZ recorded US$190.52 million in lithium sulphate sales in the nine months to September. September mineral export earnings rose 116.6 percent year on year to US$685.03 million. The industry projects about US$1 billion a year in turnover from lithium sulphate exports at full tilt. Against that, the reported claim that Prospect alone has paid over US$100 million in taxes, if accurate, would show how heavily the state is already taxing a sector it is also demanding reinvest in domestic processing. The tension is the story: Harare wants local value addition by 2027 and maximal fiscal take now, while Chinese producers who have invested about US$2 billion since 2021 say the two demands cannot both be met at current margins.
