Vice President JD Vance attributed the Republican midterm defeat directly to the war with Iran, in a lengthy post on X on Wednesday morning after Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate. "The reason we lost is the war. The president himself said so last night," Vance wrote, arguing that many voters who backed the Trump and Vance ticket in 2024 switched to Democrats in 2026. He added that anyone who wished to argue the war was worth losing the Senate for the next six years should make that case honestly, and set out four priorities voters had named: end the war, focus on the cost of living and inflation, put America first, and change the United States relationship with Israel.

The remarks put distance between Vance and the administration's own account of the war, which it has repeatedly described as a victory, even as President Trump told interviewers on Tuesday night that the war was a factor in the losses while maintaining it was a wise decision and that Republicans lost in part because he was not on the ballot. Analysts quoted by the BBC described Vance's intervention as an early positioning move ahead of a possible 2028 presidential bid.

Florida Governor Ron DeSantis offered a different autopsy the same day, describing the result as close to an extinction level event for Republicans in many parts of the country and a rejection of Republicans in Washington rather than a positive verdict on Democrats. He acknowledged that the Iran war and higher fuel prices contributed, but pointed also at the party's candidates and its record in Washington, and warned that Republicans may struggle to regain a Senate majority in 2028 or a House majority at all. DeSantis noted that conservative ballot measures passed in states where Republican candidates lost, which he read as evidence that voters rejected the party's officeholders, not its policies.

The three accounts now compete inside the party: the president's, centred on his own absence from the ballot; the vice president's, centred on the war; and DeSantis's, centred on a broader rejection of the party in Washington. None of the three has produced a policy change yet. The first institutional test is the House Republican leadership election on November 10, and the argument matters beyond the party because Vance's stated first priority, ending the war, would change United States policy in the Gulf if it became administration policy.
