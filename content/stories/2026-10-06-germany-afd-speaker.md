Tobias Rausch of the Alternative for Germany (AfD) was elected president of the Saxony-Anhalt Landtag on October 6, receiving 48 of 82 votes in a secret ballot at the chamber's inaugural sitting in Magdeburg. It is the first time a far-right politician has presided over a German state parliament since 1945; the Left party's Eva von Angern drew explicit parallels to Hermann Goring's Reichstag presidency (status: enacted).

The math is the story. The AfD holds 39 of the chamber's 83 seats after its September 6 landslide (43.8 percent, the best result in party history), and the five members of the Sahra Wagenknecht Alliance (BSW) had pledged their votes, for 44. The required absolute majority was 42. Rausch got 48, meaning at least four additional votes came from other groups. CDU leader Sven Schulze had recommended abstention; only five abstentions were cast even though the CDU faction holds 15 seats, indicating defiance from within the Christian Democrats (status: reported).

The post is more than ceremonial: the Landtag president is the parliament's most senior representative, directs its administration and swears in the Minister-President. The AfD also secured a deputy presidency for Hans-Thomas Tillschneider with 44 votes, despite the BSW having said it would not support him (status: enacted).

The domestic-intelligence context sharpens the market read: the AfD's Saxony-Anhalt chapter is classified as a "confirmed right-wing extremist" endeavor by Germany's domestic security services, and several activist groups protested outside the Landtag on Tuesday (status: reported).

This is the first crack in the cordon sanitaire at the state-presidency level, and it sets up the December vote in which the AfD's lead candidate Ulrich Siegmund seeks the Minister-President post. Watch for whether the BSW-AfD cooperation that delivered Rausch extends to that vote, and whether the CDU's federal leadership disciplines the defecting state faction.

Chancellor Friedrich Merz responded a day later, hitting out at political "extremism" at an event in Berlin on October 7. "Anyone who casts their vote for the AfD is playing the extremists' game and undermining credibility," Merz said, calling it incompatible with the values of his Christian Democratic Union.

The defeats are broader than one state. The far-left Left Party won the Berlin state election on September 20, roughly doubling its previous score on a campaign against soaring housing costs, with lead candidate Elif Eralp promising to nationalise private housing associations under the 2021 referendum mandate. Merz's approval rating has sunk to 10 percent in the ARD public-broadcaster survey, the lowest for any chancellor since the survey began in 1997.

The AfD remains a dominant force in much of eastern Germany, especially among voters disillusioned with mainstream parties, though traditional parties have pledged not to enter coalitions with it. The party fell three seats short of an absolute majority in the September 6 Saxony-Anhalt election despite winning the vote. The next major test comes in April in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany's most populous state.

The domestic squeeze matters beyond Germany's borders: a chancellor at record-low approval with both flanks gaining has less room for the EU-level compromises the bloc needs on trade, defence and fiscal rules, even as Berlin jointly pushes a tougher trade posture with Paris.
